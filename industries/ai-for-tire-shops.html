Tire shops

Can AI change a tire? No. Here is what it can actually do for a tire shop.

The mounting and balancing is a machine and a tech, and nobody is automating the bay. But a tire shop is a volume, speed, and repeat-business operation wrapped around that bay, and the wrapping is where the phones ring and the reminders do not go out.

The Straight Answer

The bay is not the surface, and pretending otherwise wastes a shop's money.

WHAT A MACHINE DOES NOT DO

Break a bead, seat a tire on a rim, balance it, torque it to spec, and hand it back. Spot a sidewall bulge, a bent wheel, or a DOT date code that means the tire is done regardless of tread.

Tell a customer that the tire they came in for is not the problem.

WHAT THE SOFTWARE SURFACE ACTUALLY IS

The office: quoting a tire by vehicle and size, booking the bay, sourcing stock, and pricing a set of four.

The demand: the customer who called for a price and never came in, the rotation that came due, the seasonal changeover that arrives all at once.

The accounts: fleet and commercial work that runs on its own cadence and its own paperwork.

The Rhythm

A tire shop runs on a rhythm with four beats.

Understanding the beats matters more than understanding the software, because every one of them has a failure mode that looks like a slow week rather than a problem.

BEAT 01

The seasonal changeover

Twice a year, in a window measured in weeks, a large share of the market wants the same thing on the same day. The shop that filled its book first finished first. The shop that did not spends the spike on the phone instead of on the bay, and the overflow walks.

BEAT 02

Walk-in volume

Flat repairs, a single replacement, a pressure question. Low value, high count, and they arrive without warning. A tire shop's front desk is often one person who is also writing the ticket, taking payment, and answering the phone.

BEAT 03

The mount-and-balance bay

The constraint. More bays or a faster machine is a capital decision; scheduling the existing bay honestly is a free one. The bay decides throughput, which makes the schedule in front of it the highest-leverage administrative object in the building.

BEAT 04

The repeat interval

Rotation, balance check, pressure, and eventually a replacement set. This is the most predictable revenue in the business and the most likely to be lost, because it is nobody's emergency and it only comes back if somebody says something.

The Touch Points

Five places a tire shop actually touches this.

Every one of them administrative. None requires anyone to put a hand on a wheel.

Quote and book by vehicle and size

The customer does not know their tire size and often reads the wrong number off the sidewall. Year, make, model, and trim handle most of it, and let the shop quote a real number instead of a range.

Load index, run-flat or not, TPMS sensors, and whether a spare is involved all change the job.

Inventory and sourcing

Which sizes move, which sit, and what a distributor can deliver today. In a seasonal spike, the difference between a booked job and a lost one is whether the tire exists.

Includes the retread and commercial truck tire side, where stocking is a different calculation.

Rotation and changeover reminders

The rotation interval and the seasonal swap are known in advance for every customer in the system. Getting those messages out on time is mechanical, and it is the most repeatable revenue in the shop.

Also covers the customer who bought a set elsewhere and just needs the swap.

Fleet and commercial accounts

A fleet runs on intervals per vehicle, purchase orders, and a relationship. What it needs is an operator who knows which unit is due and can schedule around the truck being needed Monday.

Billing terms and unit-level history matter more than the retail behaviors.

The front desk while it is busy

The defining tire shop problem: the phone rings while a customer stands at the counter with keys in hand. One of the two gets ignored, and in most shops it is the phone, which is the customer who has not spent anything yet.

Phone and counter are both live channels and they compete for the same person.

What It Fixes

Three tire shop problems, three different fixes.

These are the ones that show up in the numbers as a soft quarter rather than as a visible failure, which is exactly why they persist.

PROBLEM 01

The price call that never showed

Someone rang for a price on a set of four, got a number, and drove to whoever answered next. Nothing was wrong with the quote. The failure is that the shop has no record of the call, no vehicle attached to it, and no reason to follow up. A captured lead with a size and a vehicle is a follow-up. A verbal price is a coin flip.

PROBLEM 02

The rotation reminder that never went out

Every one of those customers was in the system with a date. The reminder did not go out because nobody had the ten minutes, and the customer rotated at the shop they drove past instead. This is the cheapest revenue in the business and the easiest to lose, because losing it never looks like anything.

PROBLEM 03

The fleet unit running past its interval

A fleet account is a recurring relationship until it is not. When a vehicle blows past its interval unnoticed, the account notices before the shop does, and the next conversation is not about tires. Interval tracking per unit is administrative work that no one has time for and that a system does not forget.

What Stays Human

A tire shop sells safety, and safety is a conversation.

This is the part of the industry that resists automation on principle rather than on capability. When a tech walks a customer out to the bay and shows them a bulge in a sidewall, or a wear bar, or a DOT date code older than the owner thinks, that is the moment the shop earns its trust and its price.

That conversation cannot be scripted into a message, and it should not be. Software's job here is narrow: make sure the customer is actually standing there to have it. A worn tire nobody looked at is not a missed upsell. It is a missed safety check.

The line

The wheel damage or safety call

A person decides what is roadworthy and explains it. This is the shop's reputation in one conversation.

The mount-and-balance decision

Run-flat handling, low-profile rims, road-force balancing, and whether a wheel is safe to re-mount. Craft and judgment, every time.

The alignment recommendation

Why the last set wore the way it did. That answer comes from someone who looked at the old tires.

The physical inspection itself

Tread depth, pressure, sidewall, TPMS. Nobody has automated a hand on a tire and nobody should pretend they have.

Two Questions Shops Ask

What software a tire shop needs, and what order to buy it in.

What software is available for tire shops?

Three categories that do different jobs. A tire point-of-sale or dealer system handles catalog lookup, pricing, and inventory across the brands the shop carries. A shop management system holds the customer, the vehicle, the ticket, and the money. A layer above both handles the demand side: the calls, the reminders, the fleet intervals, and the follow-up on quotes that went quiet. RevvOS sits in that third category.

How can I start a tire business?

That is an operations and capital question before it is a software question: bay space and a balancer, a catalog and distributor relationships, a location with parking, and a plan for how the seasonal spikes get staffed. Software choices come after the first three are settled, and the honest advice for a new shop is to run the simplest thing that holds a customer record until the volume proves which part is leaking.
