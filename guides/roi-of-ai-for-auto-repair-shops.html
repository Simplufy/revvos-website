Guide

Every ROI number you have seen for shop AI was built with somebody else's inputs.

A vendor figure assumes a missed-call rate, a close rate, an average ticket, and a recovery rate. Get those wrong and a good decision looks bad, or a bad one looks excellent. This page does not give you a number. It gives you the formula and the inputs, so the number you end up with is yours and you can defend it.

Read This Part First

Why a single ROI percentage cannot apply to your shop.

Any figure you did not compute from your own inputs describes a different building, a different labor market, and a different customer mix. That is true of numbers published by vendors, by trade publications, and by this page. We do not publish an expected ROI for a shop we have not seen.

What follows is the structure of the calculation, the inputs that decide it, and where an optimistic assumption does the most damage.

The Inputs

Five inputs decide almost the entire answer.

Each of these you can measure this week from your own phone log and your own closed tickets. None requires a new system to find out.

Missed calls per week

From your phone system's call log, not from memory. Count abandoned calls, voicemails, and after-hours rings. Owners guess low, because a call that never got answered never became a person in front of them.

Close rate on inbound calls

Of the inbound calls that reach a person, what share becomes a booked appointment. Pull it from your booking log. Do not import an industry figure, because yours is the one applied to your call volume.

Average repair order

Your own, from closed tickets. Be honest about the mix: if much of your work is oil changes, the blended average is lower than owners tend to quote, and the lower number is the correct one.

Declined lines per week

Count the individual recommendations a customer said no to, not the tickets. One declined ticket may hold three lines. If your system does not log declines individually, this is the first input you cannot measure, and that gap is itself the finding.

Average declined line value

Again your own. Declines skew toward larger work than the average ticket, because a customer says no to a four-figure job more readily than to a service. More value per line recovered, but a lower recovery rate.

The sixth input is the suppression dial: what share of each pool is actually recoverable. No honest model recovers all of it. Some missed callers have already gone elsewhere. Some declined work was declined for a reason that has not changed. If the case only works at a recovery rate you would not defend out loud, it does not work.

The Arithmetic

Three terms, written in plain language.

The calculation below is the whole model. It is deliberately unglamorous: no multiplier, no compounding curve, and no assumed growth from month to month, because those are the parts that turn a defensible estimate into a sales document.

Term 1, recovered calls:
missed calls per week x 4.33 x close rate x recovery rate x average repair order
Term 2, recovered declined work:
declined lines per week x 4.33 x recovery rate x average declined line value
Term 3, advisor time returned:
advisor hours per week on manual follow-up x 4.33 x loaded hourly cost
Monthly value = Term 1 + Term 2 + Term 3

Term 3 is not revenue, it is capacity. Returning five advisor hours a week is worth something only if those hours go into work that would not otherwise have happened; if they go into a slower morning, the term is real but smaller than it looks. Nothing here accounts for the cost side, which is deliberate and is the whole subject of the companion guide on what this actually costs. A value figure without a cost figure is half a decision.

Where The Answer Moves

Two inputs carry most of the risk of being wrong.

Term 1 multiplies four inputs together, so an optimism error in any one propagates straight through and errors in two compound. Term 3 adds its inputs once each, so it is far harder to inflate by accident and usually the smaller term.

MOST FRAGILE

Missed calls per week. The number nobody measures and everybody underestimates, and the one that multiplies into everything else.

EASIEST TO OVERSTATE

The recovery rate. It is the one input with no source document behind it, which is exactly why optimistic models lean on it hardest.

MOST DEFENSIBLE

Average repair order and line values from closed tickets. Past performance is not a forecast, but it is at least evidence.

Reading A Vendor Claim

Four questions that separate a model from a pitch.

None of these are hostile. A vendor with a real model will answer all four easily, and the answers will be the same numbers you would use.

1. What missed-call volume is this built on?

If the answer is an industry average rather than a range the model tests, the output describes an average shop, not yours.

2. What recovery rate, and why that one?

A model that assumes most of a pool comes back is not modelling, it is asserting. Ask what happens to the answer at half that rate.

3. Is the forecast compounding?

Multi-year projections that grow the benefit every year assume the first year's gains multiply. Usually they repeat.

4. Does it include the running cost?

A value figure without the subscription, the implementation, and the staff time to run it is not an ROI. It is a revenue estimate with the word ROI attached.

Applied here: we gave you the structure and refused to supply the inputs, which means we cannot hand you a return figure at all. That is the intended outcome. An honest ROI for your shop depends on numbers only you have, and anyone quoting you a percentage without asking for them is describing their reference customer.
