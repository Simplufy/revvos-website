The Lapsed List: how to win back lapsed auto repair customers in three passes

To win back lapsed auto repair customers, run the lapsed list: define lapsed from your own customers' return rhythm, pull anyone with an open complaint for a manager's call, then send the rest a short message about their own car and last visit. Below are the rules, three templates and the consent checks.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

The lapsed list is three passes through your repair orders, in the same order as the video:

  1. Your gap. Define lapsed from your own shop's return rhythm, not from a number someone else uses.
  2. The complaint pull. Take out anyone who left unhappy. They get a call from a manager, not a marketing text.
  3. The message. Write to each remaining customer about their own car, their last visit and only what the record supports.

The rest of this page is the working version: the sorting rules, three message templates you can copy, the consent checks, and how to read the results without fooling yourself.

Who this is for

Owners, service managers and lead advisors at a repair shop with at least a year or two of repair-order history in a shop management system. You need to be able to export or filter customers by last visit date, last job and any notes. You don't need a new tool. Some shop systems already have a version of this built in. Tekmetric's support documentation describes a Lost Customer Follow-Up automation with a delay you set, sent only to customers who opted in to marketing. Its launch post also mentions seasonal campaigns to bring customers back when business slows. Check what yours already does before buying anything.

The tool is rarely the problem. The list is.

Pass one sets your gap

"Lapsed" has no industry standard, so build the rule from your own customers.

  1. Pull the last 24 months of repair orders with customer, vehicle, last visit date and last job.
  2. Look at the customers who come back regularly. How long is the usual gap between their visits? In many shops the regulars return every five to seven months. Yours may differ.
  3. Set your lapsed line well past that gap. With a five to seven month rhythm, twelve months without a visit is a fair line.
  4. Treat different customers on their own clock. A tire-only customer or a once-a-year inspection customer is not lapsed at seven months.
  5. Remove anyone who can't come back: moved away, sold the car, asked you to stop contacting them.

Write the rule down in one sentence, such as "No visit in 12 months, last job was maintenance or repair, no open complaint." Two advisors should sort the same customer the same way. That sentence is the rule the software follows later, if you automate it at all.

Pass two pulls the complaints

Before anyone gets a message, filter for the last experience.

  • Open or recent complaint. A comeback, a billing dispute, a bad review or a note like "customer upset." Move these to a separate list for a manager to call. A cheerful win-back text to someone who left angry makes it worse.
  • Declined safety work. These customers are a different conversation. The message can mention the finding, plainly, but a person should decide whether a call fits better.
  • Normal last visit. These stay on the lapsed list for pass three.

The manager's call list should be short. If it isn't, that tells you something about why customers lapse that no campaign will fix.

Pass three writes the message

The weak version sounds like every other shop: "We miss you! Come back for a discount." It says nothing about the customer, and a discount teaches people to wait for the next one.

A better message names the car, the last visit and a reason that comes from the record. One ask, a real person's name, a way to reply and a way to stop.

Template 1, maintenance came due

Hi [first name], it's [advisor] at [shop]. Your [vehicle] was last in for [service] in [month]. It's probably due again by now. If you'd like us to take a look, reply here and we'll find a time that works. Reply STOP to opt out.

Template 2, a finding was noted

Hi [first name], it's [advisor] at [shop]. When your [vehicle] was in for [service] in [month], our tech noted [finding from the repair order]. If you'd like us to check it again, reply here and we'll set up a time. Reply STOP to opt out.

Use template 2 only when the repair order actually records the finding. If the file doesn't say the brakes were thin, don't write it.

Template 3, the season changed

Hi [first name], it's [advisor] at [shop]. With [season] coming, we're checking in with customers we haven't seen in a while. If your [vehicle] needs anything before then, reply here and we'll find a time. Reply STOP to opt out.

Keep each one under three sentences before the opt-out line. Read every message before the first batch goes out, even if software filled in the fields.

Consent comes before the send

Win-back messages are usually marketing, so the consent rules matter more here than for a "your car is ready" text. Marketing texts sent with automated technology generally need prior express written consent under the TCPA. The FCC's one-to-one consent rule was vacated in January 2025 and the earlier rules reinstated, per Womble Bond Dickinson. Texting from software over a standard 10-digit number also requires A2P 10DLC registration, according to Twilio's documentation.

Email has its own rules. Under the CAN-SPAM Act, a commercial email needs a working opt-out, and a request to stop has to be honored within 10 business days. Honor text opt-outs the same day.

This is general information, not legal advice. Have a lawyer confirm your consent wording and what you may send to whom.

What a person keeps

  • Writing the lapsed rule and reviewing the list before the first send.
  • Every customer on the complaint list.
  • Replies. A win-back message that gets an answer needs an advisor within hours, not days.
  • Anything with a price or a safety finding.

How to run the first batch

  1. Start with 25 to 50 customers from the lapsed list, not the whole file.
  2. Send from a named person, during business hours, when someone can answer.
  3. Track every reply in one place: booked, not now, wrong number, opted out.
  4. After two weeks, read who answered and why. Adjust the templates, then widen the batch.

How to tell it worked

Count three things: replies, booked appointments and opt-outs. If you can, keep a small group of lapsed customers you don't contact and compare, because some of them come back on their own. There are no honest industry benchmarks to borrow, and numbers on vendors' marketing pages are not evidence for your shop. Your own before and after is the only number that counts.

A rising opt-out count is a signal too. It usually means the list was too broad or the message too generic.

Questions people ask

How many months without a visit counts as lapsed?

There is no standard. Look at how often your regular customers return and set the line well past that gap. With regulars every five to seven months, twelve months is a fair line.

Should I offer a discount to bring lapsed customers back?

Usually not as the opener. A message about their own car and last visit gives a real reason, while a discount mostly teaches people to wait for the next one.

Can I text lapsed customers without their consent?

Win-back texts are usually marketing, which generally needs prior express written consent, and software texting over a 10-digit number needs A2P 10DLC registration. Have a lawyer confirm your wording.

What do I do with customers who left with a complaint?

Take them off the lapsed list and give them to a manager to call. A marketing text to someone who left unhappy tends to make things worse.
