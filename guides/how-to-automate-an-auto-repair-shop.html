Guide

How to automate a repair shop, in the order that actually works.

Most shops automate in the order their vendors call them. A presence product, then a review tool, then an ad campaign, and eighteen months later the phone still rings out at lunch. The order matters more than the software, and there is a sequence where each stage makes the next one cheaper.

The governing principle

Every automation either protects work you already have or buys work you do not.

Protecting existing work is arithmetic that works in your favor: the vehicle is already known, the customer already chose you, and the remaining obstacle is attention rather than demand. Buying new work only pays off if everything downstream of it holds.

That gives the ordering rule. Fix capture before nurture, because a reminder sent to a customer who could not reach you last week is worse than no message. Fix the work you already quoted before you buy leads, because an unanswered estimate costs nothing to chase.

The test for any proposed automation: if it stopped working tomorrow, how long would it take you to notice? If the honest answer is weeks, it is not protecting anything yet. Put it later in the sequence.

The plan

Five stages. Do not start the next one until the current one holds.

Each stage has a gate: a thing that has to be visible in your own numbers before the next stage is worth starting. The gates are deliberately unglamorous.

Stage
00

Measure for one month and change nothing

This stage produces no visible improvement and it is the one owners skip. Without it, every later decision is a guess about which problem is largest.

Four numbers, all available from systems you already pay for: inbound calls that went unanswered, the average time from a form or text to the first reply, open estimates sorted by state, and approved jobs with no appointment on the schedule.

Gate

You can name the single largest leak in the shop and put a number on it.

Stage
01

Capture: make sure nothing that reaches you is lost

Start here because everything else depends on it. A missed call is the only leak where the customer is actively choosing a shop at that minute, and where a competitor answers while you do not.

Three parts. Text back every missed call within minutes, with a question rather than an apology. Give every form, chat, and profile message a named owner rather than a shared inbox. Set a first-reply standard in minutes and hold it, because a standard in hours is not one.

Gate

A month where every inbound contact got a response, and you can prove it from the log rather than from memory.

Stage
02

Follow-through: finish the jobs you already started

This is the cheapest stage of all, because the shop already did the expensive part: diagnosed the vehicle, quoted the work, and earned the customer's attention. What is missing is a follow-up that knows what state the estimate is in.

Follow open estimates by state rather than by age, re-offer declined lines where the reason may have changed, and get every approved job onto the schedule before the customer leaves the counter. The approved-but-unscheduled job is the least visible leak in most shops and often the largest.

Gate

No estimate sits in an undecided state for more than a few days without somebody having spoken to that customer.

Stage
03

Nurture: the customers you already earned

Now that capture works and open work gets finished, keeping in touch stops being a risk. Service reminders, reactivation for customers who have gone quiet, and a review request after a completed job all belong here rather than earlier, because each one generates a reply the front desk has to be able to handle.

Trigger these from real events rather than a calendar. A reminder tied to the vehicle and its last service is a different message from a quarterly blast, and the second one teaches customers to ignore your texts.

Gate

Replies to these messages get answered inside the same day, and the opt-out requests are honored the first time.

Stage
04

Demand: buy reach last, not first

Only now does advertising make sense, and it makes more sense than it would have at the start for a simple reason: the same budget now lands on a front desk that answers, a follow-up process that works, and a schedule that does not let an approved job evaporate.

Judge every channel on booked work rather than on clicks or leads. A channel report that cannot be tied to a repair order is a subscription, not a channel.

Gate

Cost per kept job is a number you know per source, and at least one source has been cut because of it.

Nothing in this sequence requires a purchase. A shop can run stages zero through two with a phone log, a list, and somebody willing to make the calls. That is the point of the ordering: the cheapest work happens first, and it pays for what comes later. See the five leaks this sequence closes ->

The other list

Do not automate these yet.

Not never. Not at this stage. Each of these is either a judgment call, a pricing decision, or a customer-facing moment where a mistake costs more than the time it saves.

Do not automate
Why not yet
Safety judgments and whether a vehicle should be driven
Brakes, steering, overheating, a fuel smell, an airbag light. A wrong automated answer here is a liability question. The right outcome is a person telling the customer to stop driving.
Price negotiation and discounting
A tool does not know your labor rate, your margin on this job, or what you will trade away. Automated discounting trains customers to wait for the next offer.
Explaining a diagnosis in your own words
The advisor's ability to make a repair make sense is the shop's actual product. A drafted explanation is a starting point for a person, not a message that goes out alone.
Completion date and loaner promises
Only the shop knows what parts arrived and what the bays can hold. An automated promise that outruns reality creates a phone call you will lose.
Complaints, escalations, and anything said in anger
An upset customer needs a human within one message, not a sequence. Automating this is how a service complaint becomes a public review.
Insurance and DRP claim decisions
Adjuster negotiations, supplements, and documentation vary by program and claim. Get the process stable on paper before any part of it runs unattended.
Anything you have not run by hand at least once
If a person has not done the task manually and made it work, there is no process to automate, only a hope. Automating a broken process makes it fail faster and harder to see.

Honest limits

Three problems that look like automation problems and are not.

A shop nobody wants to call. If the reviews describe long waits and surprise charges, more messages will reach more people and produce the same conclusion. Fix the operating problem the reviews describe first.

No capacity to absorb the work. A shop with one empty bay and a two-week backlog does not need more leads. It needs a scheduling conversation, which is a different exercise. See capacity planning.

A team that does not trust it. An advisor who believes the system will annoy customers will quietly stop approving messages, and the process dies without anyone deciding to kill it. Start narrow and expand when they ask for more.

The useful mental model is that automation removes the excuse. Once the calls are answered and the estimates are chased, the remaining constraint is visible: usually capacity, staffing, or pricing. If you only do one thing

Stage one, this week, without buying anything.

The measurement stage is free and it decides whether any of the rest is worth your money.

Day 1

Export last month's inbound call log. Mark every call that was not answered during business hours.

Day 2

Open the form inbox, the chat tool, and the profile messages. Time how long the oldest unanswered one has sat there.

Day 3

List every open estimate. Sort into three piles: waiting on the customer, waiting on the shop, and nobody knows.

Day 4

Count the approved jobs with no appointment. This is the pile most owners have never looked at.

Day 5

Multiply each pile by your average repair order and the share you think would say yes. Pick the largest. That is stage one.
