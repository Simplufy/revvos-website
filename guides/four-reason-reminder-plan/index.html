The Four-Reason Reminder Plan: auto repair reminder templates with timing and owners

These auto repair reminder templates follow the four-reason reminder plan: an appointment reminder the day before, a service interval reminder when due and four weeks later, a phone call for declined safety work, and a check-in three to five days after pickup, each with a named owner for the reply.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

The four-reason reminder plan is four reminders, each with a reason and an owner, in the same order as the video:

  1. The appointment reminder. Sent the day before. The front desk owns the reply.
  2. The service interval. One when the service comes due and one four weeks later, then stop. An advisor owns the reply.
  3. Declined work. A safety item gets a phone call from the advisor who saw the car, not a generic text.
  4. The check-in. Three to five days after pickup, asking if the car is running right. The service manager reads every answer.

Below is the timing for each, a message you can copy for each, and the checks that keep reminders from turning into noise.

Who this is for

Owners and service managers at shops that already run a shop management system and want reminders to bring cars back without someone remembering to send them. Most systems can already send them. Tekmetric's feature page, as one example, lists appointment confirmations, pre-visit reminders, post-visit follow-ups, and service reminders for declined jobs and upcoming maintenance. The gap in most shops is not sending. It is deciding why each reminder goes out and who handles the answer.

Why bother

Drivers put maintenance off. An Auto Care Association survey of more than 100,000 households, reported by THE SHOP, put delayed auto maintenance at $24.9 billion. That was 2019 data for the whole country, so it says nothing exact about your shop. It does say that a car overdue for service is common, and that some of those customers simply weren't asked.

Before you set anything up

  • Clean the list. Remove customers who sold the car, moved away or asked you to stop. A reminder about a car someone no longer owns is the fastest way to look careless.
  • Name an owner for each type. Write the name next to each reminder below. If a reply has no owner, it sits unread.
  • Pick the channel by the reason. A sensible default is text for same-week appointments, email for interval reminders that carry detail, and a call from a person for anything with a safety item. Customers often like text. A 2022 TEXT2DRIVE survey reported by Auto Remarketing found 57% of dealership service customers preferred text for appointment reminders, though it came from a vendor and covered dealership customers, so ask your own.

Reminder one, the appointment

Timing. The day before. Add a second one the morning of only if your shop runs tight and no-shows cost you a bay.

Owner. The front desk. When someone writes back "Can I do Thursday instead?", a person answers it the same day.

Message to copy

Hi [first name], this is [shop]. A reminder that your [vehicle] is booked for [service] tomorrow at [time]. Reply here if you need to change it. Reply STOP to opt out.

Reminder two, the service interval

Timing. One when the interval comes due, by date or mileage since the last visit. One more four weeks later. Then stop. A customer who ignored two reminders won't be won over by a third, and more than two or three non-urgent reminders a year starts to train people to ignore all of them.

Owner. A service advisor.

Message to copy

Hi [first name], it's [advisor] at [shop]. Your [vehicle] had its last [service] on [date], so it's coming due. If you'd like to book it, reply here with a day that works. Reply STOP to opt out.

Reminder three, declined work

This is the one that matters most, because it often carries a safety item.

Timing. For a safety item, soon after the customer declines. A second contact at the next natural point, such as a season change or their next visit.

Owner. The advisor who saw the car.

The rule. You'd think a text is the easy way to bring up those rear brakes, but a safety finding lands very differently depending on the customer and how the last visit felt. The advisor calls. A text can follow the call to confirm what was said.

Message to copy, after the call

Hi [first name], thanks for talking with me today. As we discussed, the [finding] on your [vehicle] was noted on [date]. When you're ready, reply here and I'll set up a time. [advisor], [shop]

Only mention what the repair order records. If the tech didn't write it down, don't say it.

Reminder four, the check-in

Timing. One message, three to five days after pickup.

Owner. The service manager reads every answer, because a "not really" is a comeback that hasn't happened yet.

Message to copy

Hi [first name], it's [shop]. Is your [vehicle] running right since the [service]? Reply here if anything feels off and we'll take care of it.

What runs on its own and what a person keeps

Safe to send automatically: appointment reminders, "your vehicle is ready," and interval reminders whose wording you approved ahead of time.

Keep with a person: anything with a price, any safety recommendation, and every reply from a customer who sounds annoyed or confused.

Consent and opt-outs

Texting from software over a standard 10-digit number needs registration. Twilio's documentation says such messages must be registered for A2P 10DLC. Marketing-type messages also need the right consent, and the FCC's one-to-one consent rule was vacated in 2025 with the earlier rules reinstated. Where a service reminder ends and marketing begins depends on the facts and your state. This is general information, not legal advice; have a lawyer confirm your wording. STOP should work on every channel, and the shop honors it the same day.

Mistakes that make reminders worse

  • No reason. "Your vehicle is due for service" says nothing. Name the car and the item.
  • No owner. The customer replies and nobody sees it for three days.
  • Too many. Past two or three a year on non-urgent items, people tune out.
  • Texting a safety finding cold. Call first.

How to tell it worked

Before you switch anything on, count last month's booked appointments that came from a reminder, and how many replies went unanswered for more than a day. After a month on the plan, count both again. Look at no-shows for reminded appointments and at how many check-in replies turned into a fix before the customer called you. Those are your numbers. Nobody else's average fits your shop.

Questions people ask

How many service reminders should a customer get?

For the interval reminder, one when it comes due and one four weeks later, then stop. More than two or three non-urgent reminders a year tends to train people to ignore all of them.

Should declined brake work be followed up by text?

For a safety item, the advisor who saw the car should call. A text can follow the call to confirm what was discussed.

Who should answer replies to automated reminders?

Name an owner for each type: the front desk for appointments, an advisor for intervals and declined work, and the service manager for post-visit check-ins.

Do I need consent to text service reminders?

Texting from software over a 10-digit number needs A2P 10DLC registration, and marketing-type messages need the right consent. Where reminders end and marketing begins depends on the facts, so have a lawyer confirm your wording.
