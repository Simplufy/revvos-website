Supplements

The supplement is where a collision shop wins or loses, and it is the least automated step in the shop.

Everything upstream of the supplement has a system. The estimate has an estimating platform, the parts order has a vendor portal, the schedule has a board. The supplement, which decides whether the shop is paid for the work it actually performed, is usually a stack of photos in a phone and an email written from memory at the end of the day. That is the gap this page is about.

See it on your supplements -> The estimate lifecycle

Why It Exists

The estimate is a prediction written before the car was opened.

Nobody is at fault for that. An estimator writing on a Tuesday is working from photographs, a scan, and a walk-around. The teardown happens days later, and only then does the vehicle say what it actually needs. The supplement is the mechanism the industry built to reconcile the two, and it exists precisely because the first document cannot be right.

Before teardown

The prediction

Written from what is visible. Damage on the outer panel, a scan that may or may not have thrown a code, a part that looks replaceable, a repair that looks like a repair.

Everything here is an inference. It is also the number the customer hears and the insurer approves first.

After teardown

The truth

What the vehicle actually needs once the bumper is off, the liner is out, and the apron is measured. Hidden damage, a bracket nobody predicted, a calibration the repair now requires, a part that will not fit the trim.

Every difference between these two columns is a supplement line, and every one of them is labor and material the shop has already committed to performing.

The Triggers

Five things that mean a supplement is required.

Four of the five are discoverable by someone at the shop, and the fifth arrives from the customer. All five have the same property: the approved sheet no longer describes the repair in front of you.

TRIGGER 01

Hidden damage

Found by: teardown

Underneath the panel the estimate was written against, there is more: a reinforcement, an apron, a rail, a mounting point, corrosion. This is the most common supplement trigger and the one that most often arrives late, because the teardown may not happen for days after the customer has already been given a number.

Documentation needed: photos of the damage with the panel off, the measurement or the procedure reference that supports the repair method, and the labor time for the added operation.

TRIGGER 02

A part that does not fit

Found by: the technician, mid-install

The right part number, the wrong actual part: a different trim level, a bracket that does not align, a lamp assembly with the wrong connector, an aftermarket panel whose gaps will not close. The estimate was correct on paper and wrong on the vehicle.

Documentation needed: the part number ordered against the part number the vehicle needs, a photo of the mismatch, and the cost difference if the correct part is more expensive.

TRIGGER 03

An OEM procedure discovered mid-job

Found by: the repair plan, mid-repair

The manufacturer requires a step the estimate does not carry: a sectioning procedure rather than a replacement, a corrosion protection application after a weld, a scan after a repair, a one-time-use fastener that must be replaced rather than reused. The shop cannot skip it and cannot absorb it.

Documentation needed: the procedure reference, the position statement or repair document that requires it, and the labor time and materials the step adds.

TRIGGER 04

A calibration the original estimate missed

Found by: the repair plan, at reassembly

The vehicle has a camera, radar or sensor behind work that is now being done, and the manufacturer requires a recalibration once it is reassembled. It is a line item, a sublet booking, and a stage in the repair all at once, and it is the single most common place a supplement goes missing.

Documentation needed: the equipment the vehicle carries, the procedure that requires the calibration, and the sublet cost. See where calibration sits in the estimate.

TRIGGER 05

A customer add

Found by: the customer, mid-repair

A pre-existing dent on an adjacent panel, a scratch the owner now wants addressed while the car is already in the booth, a request to keep a replaced part. The shop is willing, the car is already apart, and the work is outside the approved scope.

Documentation needed: a photo of the added damage, written confirmation from the customer that the work was requested, and whether it is charged to the customer or the claim.

The Timing Problem

A supplement raised late costs more than a supplement raised wrong.

Two cars, the same hidden damage, the same evidence. The only difference is the day the supplement is raised, and that difference is paid in rental days and in a bay the car should have left.

DAY 1

Raised on the day it was found

D1   teardown, hidden damage found
D1   photos, procedure, part, labor time captured
D2   supplement submitted with the packet attached
D2   appraiser reviews a complete file

The appraiser sees the same evidence the technician saw, close enough to the discovery that the question is about the repair rather than about the delay. The car keeps its place in the schedule.

DAY 6

Raised after the car stalled

D1   teardown, hidden damage found
D1   photos taken, nobody assembles them
D3   car sits, tech moves to another job
D6   supplement written from memory

Six rental days, a bay occupied by a car nobody can finish, and a packet reconstructed after the fact, which is exactly when a missing photo turns into a line the shop does not get paid for. See cycle time software for the scheduling side of this.

The documentation a supplement needs, every time: photos of the damage, the OEM procedure reference that supports the repair method, the part number and what it costs, and the labor time justification. Four items. A supplement missing any one of them is a conversation the shop has twice.

The Line

What automation handles, and what a person owns.

The supplement is the step where the boundary matters most, because it is the step where money is agreed. Split it cleanly.

Automation
  • Assemble the packet: photos, procedure reference, part number, labor time, in one place and attached to the right vehicle
  • Flag likely missing items by reading the repair plan against the approved sheet, including a calibration the first estimate did not carry
  • Track the supplement's status and its age, so one does not sit for six days inside a busy week
  • Show a manager which cars are waiting on a supplement and how much rental time each day of delay represents
Your people
  • The appraiser conversation: what the shop is asking for and why
  • The negotiation when a line is disputed, including what the shop will concede
  • The decision that a supplement is warranted at all, and what it should contain
  • The sign-off. A person's name goes on the supplement, and that person answers for it

That line is not a limitation to work around. A supplement is a request for money from an insurer, backed by a claim that the work was necessary, and it is answered by a person who has to be able to defend it. Automation that negotiated on a shop's behalf would be automating exactly the part of this business that depends on judgment, relationships and accountability. Assembling the evidence is the hard, repetitive, forgettable half of the job. That half is worth automating.
