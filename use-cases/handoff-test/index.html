The Handoff Test: an auto repair workflow automation checklist for your seven handoffs

This auto repair workflow automation checklist is the handoff test: three questions you ask about each of the seven handoffs around a repair order. Does it happen every day, is it a message or a status change rather than a judgment, and what does a miss cost? The handoff with three yeses and the most waits is the one to automate first, while pricing and diagnoses stay with your people.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

The three questions, in the order the video asks them

The handoff test is three questions you ask about one handoff at a time. A handoff is any moment where a car's next step depends on someone noticing that the last step is done.

  1. Does it happen every day? A handoff that comes up a dozen times a day is worth more attention than one that comes up once a month.
  2. Is it a message or a status change, not a judgment? "The part arrived" and "the estimate is still open" are facts a system can see. "This brake job should cost this much" is a judgment, and it stays with a person.
  3. What does a miss cost? A car that waits is a bay that is not billing, an advisor who gets a second phone call, or a customer who drives off to the shop down the road.

A handoff that gets three yeses is your first thing to automate. One that fails the second question stays with your people no matter how often it happens.

Who this sheet is for

It is for the owner or service manager of an independent repair shop, or of a small group with two or three locations, who wants to try workflow automation without buying something that tries to run the whole shop. You do not need new software to score your handoffs. You need a week of attention, the repair orders you already have, and one person willing to write things down.

What you need first

  • Your shop management system's job board or RO list. Tekmetric, for example, describes a job board that shows every job in real time, with technicians updating status from their own devices. Any system that shows where a repair order is today will do.
  • Last week's repair orders. You will count how long cars sat between steps.
  • A named owner for each stage. If nobody owns the estimate follow-up, a faster reminder reaches nobody.
  • A copy of the scoring table below, printed or in a spreadsheet.

The seven shop handoffs

These are the handoffs around a typical repair order. Tekmetric's description of the RO lifecycle is a good map: an advisor opens the RO with the customer and vehicle history, inspection results attach to it, the estimate flows into it when the customer approves, and it becomes the invoice when the job is done. Between each of those steps sits a handoff.

# Handoff What a miss looks like
1 Intake to inspection The car is checked in but no tech is assigned
2 Inspection to estimate Photos are taken but the estimate is not built
3 Estimate to approval The estimate is sent and nobody follows up
4 Approval to parts Work is approved but parts are not ordered
5 Parts to repair The part arrives and the car stays parked
6 Repair to pickup The work is done and the customer is not called
7 Pickup to follow-up The car leaves and no reminder is set

How to score each handoff

Count a high cost of a miss as a yes. Medium is a maybe. Low is a no.

Step by step

  1. Pull last week's repair orders. For each one, note when it entered each stage and when it left.
  2. Mark every wait longer than your own limit. Pick a limit per stage. For parts, an older Ratchet+Wrench consultant piece suggests a maximum local parts wait of one to two hours; for an estimate with no reply, many shops use a day or two.
  3. Answer the three questions for each handoff. Write Y or N. Don't argue about edge cases; go with what happened last week.
  4. Circle the handoff with three yeses and the most waits. That is your first target. If two tie, take the one a customer would notice first.
  5. Name the owner and the time limit, for example "no estimate older than two days without a follow-up, owned by the front-counter advisor."
  6. Let a tool watch for the limit and prepare the next step. The tool flags the item and drafts the message; the owner reads it, edits it if needed and sends it.
  7. Review after two weeks. Keep the flags that led to a decision and drop the ones everyone ignored.

A worked example (demo shop)

Here is how the sheet might look for Redline Auto, a made-up shop used for illustration only. Its front counter found that estimates sat after they were sent. Say one F-150 brake estimate went out and nobody heard back for eight days. That handoff happens every day, the next step is a message rather than a judgment, and a quiet estimate is a job the shop may lose. Three yeses.

The parts-to-repair handoff scored three as well. AutoVitals describes tools that reassign technicians in real time when a job finishes, extra work is approved or a part arrives, which is exactly that handoff. Redline picked the estimate first because the customer feels it directly, and kept parts as its second target.

Pricing the brake job never made the sheet. It fails the second question.

What to keep with people

Some steps look like handoffs but are really decisions. Keep these with a person:

  • Pricing and discounts. A system can show the numbers; the price is a call.
  • Diagnoses. Nothing should tell a customer what is wrong before the technician has confirmed it.
  • Warranty approvals and goodwill. These depend on the customer and the history.
  • Who works on what, when it is a judgment. Reassigning a tech when a part lands is a handoff. Deciding which tech is right for a tricky electrical job is a judgment.

The shops that run well tend to have clear owners at every stage. One shop in a Ratchet+Wrench profile had technicians write their own repair orders with advisors spot-checking them, and advisors sending customer updates twice a day. Automation on top of habits like that helps. Automation on top of unclear ownership just moves the confusion faster.

What to watch out for

  • Automating a broken handoff. If the estimate process is messy, a reminder makes it messy sooner. Fix the owner and the limit first.
  • Starting with the customer text and skipping the internal status. The customer feels the internal stall before any message goes out.
  • Sending without a person. Keep drafts as drafts until you have watched a workflow for a few weeks and decided otherwise.
  • Flags without a source. Each flag should say which system it came from and when that system last updated, so your advisor can trust it.
  • Waits you can't see. FenderBender's advice to keep technicians supplied with parts, supplies, approvals, tooling and training so they do not wait is a reminder that some waits never show up on a job board. Walk the floor once a day during the test week.

How to tell it worked

After two weeks, compare the circled handoff with the week you scored:

  • Fewer items waited past your limit.
  • The owner can name every open item without searching.
  • The drafts that went out were edited lightly, not rewritten.
  • Nobody asked "did anyone call them back?" at the counter.

If those hold, score the sheet again and move to the next handoff with three yeses. If they don't, the problem is usually the owner or the limit, not the tool.

Questions people ask

What is a handoff in an auto repair shop?

Any point where a car's next step depends on someone noticing the last one is done: the estimate is sent, the part has arrived, the work is finished. Each one is a place where a car can sit.

Which handoff should most shops automate first?

Score your own week first. Estimates waiting for approval often score three yeses because they happen daily, the next step is a message, and a quiet estimate can become a lost job.

Should the automated follow-up send by itself?

Start with drafts. The tool flags the item and prepares the message, and your advisor reads, edits and sends it. Decide on automatic sending later, one workflow at a time.

Do I need new software to use the handoff test?

No. The sheet works with the repair orders and job board you already have. Software helps once you know which handoff you want it to watch.
