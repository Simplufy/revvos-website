Estimating

Collision estimate automation touches the assembly, never the judgment.

The estimate is not a form. It is a document with a lifecycle that starts with photos on a lot and ends as a bill, and it changes shape at every stage in between. Automation can make each stage faster to assemble and harder to get wrong. Which line items belong on the sheet, whether the repair is safe, and what gets signed are still decisions a qualified person makes.

See it on a real estimate -> Collision overview

The Five Stages

An estimate has five stages, and automation behaves differently in each one.

The mistake shops make is treating "estimate automation" as one purchase. It is five different problems that happen to share a document, and the further down this rail you go, the narrower the automation gets and the wider the human's role becomes.

STAGE 01

Pre-scan and photos at intake

Automation assembles and flags

Collect the photos and any pre-scan output against the right vehicle, name them by corner and side, and flag gaps. Missing angles, a photo too dark to read damage from, no shot of the VIN plate, and no odometer reading are all mechanically detectable.

The human line

Whether the photos show what they need to show, and what the vehicle needs next. A camera can confirm it captured twelve images. Only an estimator can say the twelfth one is useless.

STAGE 02

The initial estimate, from photos and a teardown

Automation assembles and flags

Pull the vehicle into the right guide, carry the intake photos and the pre-scan codes into the sheet, and check the draft against what the shop has already recorded. Missing line items against the teardown photos, a part that matches the wrong trim or option package, and a photo that suggests hidden damage behind a panel are the catches worth having.

The human line

The line items, the repair method, and whether the structure is repairable or replaceable. Two estimators looking at the same dent can write two defensible estimates. That difference is judgment, and it is the job.

STAGE 03

The insurer review and the appraisal

Automation assembles and flags

Have the documentation ready before the appraiser arrives: the photos, the teardown evidence, the part numbers, the OEM procedure reference, and the labor time justification for each contested line. Flag the lines where the shop's sheet and the insurer's draft disagree, so the conversation starts from a list instead of a memory.

The human line

The negotiation. Everything about this stage is a conversation between people: what the shop will accept, what it will argue, and where it will hold. Software can prepare that conversation. It cannot have it.

STAGE 04

The supplement, when hidden damage is found

Automation assembles and flags

Detect that the repair has moved past the approved sheet, assemble the teardown evidence, and keep the request's age visible so it does not sit for a week while the car occupies a bay. This stage is covered in full on collision supplement automation.

The human line

The request itself and the sign-off. A supplement is a person asking an insurer for money with a document behind it, and that person has their name on the outcome.

STAGE 05

The final bill

Automation assembles and flags

Reconcile the approved sheet, the supplements, the sublet invoices and the parts actually received against what is being billed. Flag the gap: labor performed and never claimed, a sublet invoice not yet on the file, a betterment or deductible line that does not match the approved version.

The human line

What the customer is told about the bill, what gets written off, and what the shop is willing to defend if it is questioned later. A reconciliation report does not decide who pays for a surprise.

What Automation Is Actually Good At

Four flags worth catching before a person signs anything.

None of these decide anything. Each one hands an estimator a question they would rather be asked at the desk than discover at the end of the job.

FLAG 01

A missing line item

The teardown evidence or the photos support work that is not on the sheet. A blend panel, a hardware replacement, a corrosion protection step, a scan after the repair.

FLAG 02

A photo that suggests hidden damage

A gap, a ripple, or a misalignment visible in an image that the current sheet does not account for. Worth a second look before the panel goes back on.

FLAG 03

A procedure the OEM requires

The manufacturer specifies a step, a scan, a calibration or a replacement condition that the draft estimate does not carry. Position statements and repair procedures are the reference; the flag just makes sure someone checked.

FLAG 04

A parts choice that conflicts with the rules

An aftermarket or recycled option selected where the vehicle's age, the OEM guidance or the insurer's own written rules point somewhere else. Sometimes the shop wants that argument. It should have it deliberately.

The rule that keeps this honest: a flag is a question, not an edit. Automation that silently adds or removes a line from an estimate is not automating the estimate, it is writing it, and the person who signs has lost control of the document.

An Honest Note On ADAS

ADAS in estimates is real, it is emerging, and it is not something we claim to do.

The genuine development in this category is around driver assistance systems. Vehicles increasingly require a calibration or a scan after common collision repairs, and the question of whether that work has been captured is exactly the kind of question automation is suited to. Estimates and repair plans are now being reviewed against the vehicle's ADAS equipment, so a repair that involves a camera, radar or sensor behind a panel carries a calibration the original sheet may not mention.

That work is being done inside the estimating and insurance platforms, by the vendors who own the vehicle data and the procedure libraries. It is a real direction for the industry and it is worth watching. It is also not a RevvOS capability, and this page will not pretend otherwise.

What we do is narrower and it sits beside that work: read the repair plan the shop is already running, and make sure the calibration line, the sublet booking and the reassembly order are all visible to the person who decides when the car is finished. The calibration question itself belongs to the estimator and the OEM procedure, and to the platform holding them.

Where This Sits

The estimate is one document in a longer flow.

Stage four above, the supplement, is where collision shops win or lose, and it deserves its own treatment: see collision supplement automation.

What happens to the car after the estimate is approved is a scheduling problem, and the clock that matters most to the customer is the one between drop-off and pickup. That is collision cycle time software.

The work that moves a car through the shop around the estimate, including the intake, parts and sublet clocks that decide whether an estimate ever becomes a finished repair, is on auto body shop automation software.
