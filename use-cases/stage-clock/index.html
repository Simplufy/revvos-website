The Stage Clock: stage limits and update texts for collision repair cycle time

Collision repair cycle time stretches mostly in the waits between stages, not in the panel work, and the Stage Clock is a simple way to see those waits. Give every car a clock for the stage it's in, set a limit for each stage, and watch the four places the clock usually runs out: the supplement, parts, the calibration and the customer update, each with a named person who acts on it.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

This is the guide from our video on AI for auto body shops. The video named four places where a car's clock runs out. Here they are in the same order, each with the owner who acts on it:

  1. The supplement. Flag any supplement that's been waiting past your limit. Your estimator chases the appraiser.
  2. Parts. Name every car blocked on a part before the morning meeting. A person picks OEM, aftermarket or recycled.
  3. The calibration. Book the sublet slot against the car's stage. A person signs off the calibration.
  4. The update. Text the status before the customer calls. A new delivery date is always a person's call.

Below are the stage limits to fill in, the daily check, and the status texts to copy.

Why the waits matter more than the work

In J.D. Power's 2025 U.S. Auto Claims Satisfaction Study, the average cycle time for repairable vehicles was 19.3 days, down from 22.3. The same study found cars with three or more affected driver assistance features averaged 21.5 days. Rentals run alongside: Enterprise put collision-related length of rental at 15.9 days in Q4 2025.

Most of those days aren't spent with a tech's hands on the car. They go to a supplement waiting on an appraiser, a part with no arrival date, a sublet slot nobody booked, or a decision nobody chased. A production board shows where each car is. A stage clock shows how long it's been there.

Step 1. Set a limit for every stage

Write your own limits. These are the stages most boards use, and the right-hand column is yours to fill in with your production manager. Use your own history, not a number from someone else's shop.

Stage Your limit (days or hours) Who gets the flag
Intake to teardown estimator
Teardown and blueprint estimator
Supplement waiting on approval estimator
Parts waiting parts person
Frame and body production manager
Refinish paint lead
Reassembly production manager
Calibration and sublet production manager
Quality control and detail production manager

Then give every car a clock that starts when it enters a stage and resets when it leaves. A whiteboard with a date written on each car works. So does a column in your production report.

Step 2. The four run-out points

The supplement

Hidden damage found at teardown is normal in collision work, not an exception. The supplement goes to an appraiser who may adjust it, and the car waits. When a supplement passes your limit, the estimator gets the flag and makes the call. The software can track how long it's been waiting and keep the photos attached to the right job. The supplement itself, the justification and the appraiser relationship stay with the estimator.

Parts

Before the morning production meeting, list every car blocked on a part: the part, the vendor, and the expected arrival time, or "no ETA" when there isn't one. A car with no ETA is the one to chase first. The choice between OEM, aftermarket and recycled parts, and the conversation with the customer about it, stays with a person, because those are liability decisions tied to the estimate and the repair procedure.

The calibration

This is the one most shops miss, because it often shows up late. In CCC's first-quarter 2025 data for DRP shops, 56.5% of calibrations appeared on a supplement, and repairs with multiple calibrations averaged more than 17 days keys to keys compared to 13 days. By the third quarter, 35.6% of DRP estimates included at least one calibration, and 51.5% of calibrations showed up on supplements.

So the rule is simple. The moment a calibration appears on an estimate or a supplement, book the sublet slot against the stage the car will be in, usually after reassembly. Don't wait until the car is done and then look for a slot. The calibration sign-off and its documentation stay with the person doing it, because a calibration recorded wrong is a safety problem, not a scheduling one.

The update

Customers in a rental judge the shop by how informed they feel. Send the status before they call for it, on a set rhythm and whenever a stage changes. The update never sets a new delivery date. That's a person's call, and it comes with a phone call when a supplement or a part pushes the date.

Step 3. The daily check

Run this at the start of the day, before the production meeting:

  1. Sort the board by time in stage, longest first.
  2. Mark every car past its stage limit.
  3. For each marked car, write which of the four run-out points it's stuck on.
  4. Put a name on each one: estimator, parts, production manager or front desk.
  5. Check the calibration list: every car with a calibration has a booked slot.
  6. Check the update list: every customer has had a status text since their car last changed stage.
  7. At the meeting, go through the marked cars only.

Status texts to copy

Edit the wording to your shop's voice. Get consent for texting the way your state and carrier rules require, and always honor a stop reply.

  • Stage change: "Hi, it's Redline Auto. Your vehicle moved to paint today. Next update on Thursday. Reply here with any questions."
  • Waiting on a supplement: "We found some damage at teardown that wasn't visible before, and we've sent it to your insurer for approval. We'll update you as soon as we hear back."
  • Waiting on a part: "Your vehicle is waiting on one part. We're checking the arrival date with the supplier and will update you tomorrow."
  • Calibration booked: "Your vehicle needs a camera calibration after reassembly. It's booked for Thursday, and it's part of returning the car to the maker's specifications."
  • Ready: "Your vehicle is ready for pickup. Our hours are 8 to 5. Reply to set a time."

Any text that changes the delivery date goes out only after a person calls the customer.

How to tell it's working

After a few weeks, the list of cars past their limit should get shorter, and the reasons should shift from "nobody chased it" to real constraints like a back-ordered part. Track one number for yourself: how many cars are past their stage limit each morning. If the calibration line keeps showing up, book slots earlier. If the supplement line does, look at your teardown photos and how fast they reach the appraiser.

Questions people ask

What is a good collision repair cycle time?

It depends on the mix of work, but for context J.D. Power's 2025 study put the average for repairable vehicles at 19.3 days. Set your own stage limits from your own history and track how many cars are past them each morning.

Why do calibrations stretch cycle time?

They often show up late. CCC's early 2025 data found most DRP calibrations appeared on a supplement, and repairs with multiple calibrations ran longer keys to keys. Booking the sublet slot as soon as the calibration appears avoids waiting after reassembly.

Should AI send customer updates for a body shop?

It can send routine status texts on a set rhythm and when a stage changes. A new delivery date should come from a person, with a phone call.

Who should own a stalled supplement?

The estimator. Software can track how long it has waited and keep the photos attached, but the justification and the appraiser relationship are human work.
