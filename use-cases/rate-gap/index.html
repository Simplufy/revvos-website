The Rate Gap: how to calculate effective labor rate and find where your margin leaks

Here is how to calculate effective labor rate: divide the labor revenue you collected last month by the labor hours you sold, then put the result next to your posted rate. The difference per hour times your sold hours is the rate gap in dollars. Sort your discounts by advisor and reason to see where it comes from, and keep the pricing decision with the owner.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

The rate gap, in the order the video shows it

The rate gap is the distance between two numbers every shop has: the labor rate on your sign and the labor rate you actually collect. Most margin leaks live in that distance, and they rarely come from one big mistake. They come from a lot of reasonable decisions added up over a month.

  1. Pull your effective labor rate. Tekmetric describes effective labor rate as what the shop actually earns per labor hour, after discounts, flat-rate jobs and packages, not the posted rate.
  2. Put it next to your posted rate. The difference per hour, times the hours you sold, is the gap in dollars.
  3. Sort the discounts by advisor and reason. That tells you which part of the gap is a policy, which part is a habit, and which part is a package that runs long.

The pricing decision that follows stays with you. The worksheet only shows the gap and where it comes from.

Who this worksheet is for

Owners and service managers of independent shops, or of groups with a few locations, who have a busy shop and a smaller bank balance than the bays suggest. You need about an hour, last month's repair orders and the discount report from your shop system. No new software.

How to calculate effective labor rate

The formula is short:

Effective labor rate = labor revenue collected ÷ labor hours sold

Use the same month for both numbers. Labor revenue is what customers paid for labor after discounts, not the list price. Labor hours sold are the hours billed on repair orders. Most shop systems report both; if yours reports effective labor rate directly, write down how it defines it, so you compare like with like each month.

The worksheet

Copy this into a spreadsheet. The first block is the gap; the second shows where it comes from.

Labor revenue collected last month 19,000
Labor hours sold last month 200
Effective labor rate (revenue ÷ hours) 95
Gap per hour (posted minus effective) 25
Gap for the month (gap per hour × hours sold) 5,000

The example is the one in the video and is made up for illustration. It is not a benchmark. In that example the shop collects 95 dollars on a posted 120, a gap of 25 dollars an hour, which on 200 sold hours is five thousand dollars a month.

The two tables should roughly add up. If the second one explains far less than the first, the missing part is usually labor time logged wrong or repair orders left open.

Step by step

  1. Pick last month. A full calendar month gives you enough jobs to see a pattern.
  2. Write down your posted rate. If you have more than one (diagnostic, electrical, heavy line), do one worksheet per rate.
  3. Pull labor revenue collected and labor hours sold. Calculate the effective rate and the gap.
  4. Pull the discount report. Sort it by advisor, then by reason. Count how many discounts had no reason at all.
  5. Pull package and flat-rate jobs. For each, compare the hours billed with the hours the tech actually worked.
  6. Fill in the second table. Put dollars next to each source.
  7. Decide one change. A discount rule, a package price review, a talk with one advisor. One change a month is plenty.
  8. Repeat next month and compare the gap with this one.

How to read the result

Compare the gap with your own last few months before comparing it with anyone else's. Outside numbers are context only. For example, a 2023 Ratchet+Wrench piece labeled sponsored content describes gross profit on labor as job revenue minus the cost of labor, excluding overhead, with 60 to 69% given as an industry average. Shops define labor cost differently, so treat that as a reference point, not a grade.

When Ratchet+Wrench asked owners in its 2025 survey about gross profit margin, 38% reported 50 to 59% and 7% said they do not track it. That is a spread of answers from shops that chose to respond. Your own trend tells you more.

There is also a hint that tracking travels with better numbers. In Ratchet+Wrench's 2018 survey, 44% of shops that tracked KPIs reported an effective labor rate above 90 dollars, compared with 15% of those that did not. That is a correlation, and it does not prove that tracking caused the difference.

What to watch out for

  • Extreme numbers usually mean bad data. A 2016 FenderBender article calls a labor margin of 88% "too good to be true" and 18% "really bad", and the lesson holds for the rate gap: a gap of zero, or a gap bigger than the posted rate, points to a data problem first.
  • Open repair orders. Jobs that are done but not closed make collected revenue look low.
  • Mixed rates. Averaging a diagnostic rate with a maintenance rate hides where the gap really is.
  • Blaming the advisor first. Many discounts follow a rule someone set years ago. Check the rule before the person.
  • Treating every discount as a leak. A loyalty discount that keeps a fleet account may be worth every dollar. The worksheet shows the cost; you decide whether it is worth it.

What stays with you

  • Your labor rate and your parts matrix.
  • Discount rules and who may give them.
  • Whether a thin job is a mistake or a good customer.
  • Reading net profit with your accountant, after overhead.

Software can do the arithmetic every month, rank jobs from thinnest to richest and flag a jump in discounts. It cannot make those calls for you.

How to tell it worked

After two or three months:

  • The gap per hour is smaller, or you can explain every dollar of it.
  • Discounts with no reason are close to zero.
  • Package jobs that ran over have been repriced or retimed.
  • You can say in one sentence where the margin goes.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between posted and effective labor rate?

The posted rate is what you charge per hour on paper. The effective rate is what you actually collect per labor hour after discounts, packages and flat-rate jobs. The gap between them is where much of the margin goes.

How often should I calculate effective labor rate?

Once a month is enough for most shops. Use a full calendar month so a few odd jobs don't swing the result.

Is a discount always a leak?

No. A discount that keeps a good fleet or family account can be worth it. The worksheet shows what each discount costs so you can decide which ones to keep.

Can software fix the rate gap?

Software can calculate the gap and flag changes every month. Deciding the labor rate, the discount rules and how to coach an advisor stays with the owner.
