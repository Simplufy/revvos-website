Use case

Auto body customer update automation, defined properly.

Search this phrase and you find two Facebook posts, one of them a joke about robots getting grease under their fingernails. There is no page that answers the question. So start at the beginning: a collision customer is not a mechanical customer, and a body shop's update problem is its own thing.

Map your update points -> Repair status automation

Why the body shop is different

Four things make this customer harder than a service customer.

A person waiting on an oil change loses an afternoon. A person waiting on a collision repair loses their car for a week or three, is driving a rental they did not plan for, has an insurer in the middle of the transaction, and is quietly worried that the car will never look right again. That last one is specific to body work, and it is the reason silence lands so badly.

Duration

The job runs for days or weeks, not hours. There are many natural points where a person would want to hear something.

The rental

The customer is in a rental with an expiry date. Every day of delay is a thing they have to solve with somebody else.

The insurer

A third party authorizes much of the work. The customer often cannot tell whose desk a delay is sitting on.

The result

Nobody else sees the car during the repair. The customer is trusting a claim about a finish they have not laid eyes on.

Put those together and the arithmetic of silence is simple. A customer who has heard nothing for four days assumes the worst, because the only information available to them is the absence of information. Then they call the shop, and an advisor spends fifteen minutes rebuilding trust that one message would have kept intact.

The nine update points

Your shop already has these moments.

Automation here is not inventing a new cadence. It is noticing that a collision job already produces nine moments where a customer would want to hear something, and making sure each one actually goes out. Three things belong in every one of them: the real status, the real driver of any delay, and the next date you are genuinely confident about.

01

Vehicle checked in

Confirm the car is on site, who has it, and what happens next. This is the message that ends the "did they get my car" calls.

02

Teardown found more

Hidden damage is the single most common source of customer shock in collision. Say it early, say what it means, and say it without implying the repair is out of control.

03

Supplement submitted

Tell the customer that additional work has gone to the insurer for approval, and be clear that the shop does not decide that outcome.

04

Insurer approved

The authorization landed and the job moves. This is the moment to restate the revised completion window, not to bury it in a status line.

05

In paint

A stage the customer understands and can picture. Refinish is also where a booth schedule can move, so the next date matters more than the stage name.

06

In reassembly

The car is going back together. If a sublet or a backordered part is still open, name it here rather than letting it surface as a silent extension.

07

In calibration

If an ADAS calibration or a scan is required, the customer should hear it as a planned step tied to their vehicle, not as an extra charge appearing at the end.

08

In QC

The car is being checked before it is called done. This update buys patience for a day that would otherwise feel like another delay.

09

Ready, and delivery scheduled

The best message in the sequence, and the one that should also carry the practical detail: payment, the rental return, and the time somebody will walk the customer around the car.

Two rules and three exceptions

Never promise a date you cannot keep.

The first rule is the one that decides whether this helps or hurts. An update that says "should be done Friday" and then is not done Friday is worse than no update at all, because the customer now has a broken commitment to be angry about. A good update distinguishes between what is scheduled, what is waiting, and what is merely hoped for, and it only gives a date the shop is willing to stand behind.

Rule one: the real status, not a comforting one

If the car is waiting on a supplement approval, say so. Customers accept waiting; they do not accept discovering that they were told something softer than the truth.

Rule two: the real driver of the delay

A parts backorder, a sublet, an approval still pending, a booth queue. Naming the driver is what stops an anxious customer from assuming the shop has simply forgotten their car.

Three conversations that need a person, immediately.

Automation can carry a routine update. It must hand off the moment the message stops being routine, and these three are the ones that matter.

A total loss

The car is not being repaired. A person delivers that, in their own words, and it is a person who answers the questions that follow.

A delay caused by a supplement dispute

The shop and the insurer do not agree on a line item. That conversation is between the shop and the carrier, and the customer should hear about it from a person who can explain what is actually being discussed.

A customer who is unhappy with the result

A finish complaint, a fit issue, a return visit. This is a relationship moment, and a scripted message from a queue will make it worse.

What must never be automated.

  • - The total-loss conversation with the owner of the car.
  • - Any liability question. Fault is between the insurers and the drivers, and the shop is not a party to it.
  • - A warranty complaint about work the shop performed.
  • - A promise of a completion date that the shop has not confirmed.
