Declined work follow up software: sort the "no" before you follow up

Declined work follow up software works when each declined item has a reason (money, timing, second opinion or no) and an urgency, and the reminder is timed to that reason and tied to the car's next visit. Drafts go through a person, and a firm no stays closed.

Updated October 16, 2026 · 5 min read

Declined work follow up software has to answer a question most shops never write down: when a customer says "not today," what exactly are you waiting for? If the answer is "nothing," the work is gone. If the answer is "payday," "after the trip" or "when the tires wear out," then you have a list worth keeping.

This page covers estimates that have been turned down or put off. For quotes still waiting on an answer, see auto repair estimate follow up software.

Why declined work is its own problem

Declining is common. PartsTech's 2026 survey of nearly 700 shops found that 50% said customers are delaying or declining work, 21% said customers pick lower-cost parts and services when they can, and 49% said customers are more cautious about non-required work than the year before.

Not every "no" is the same, and much of it is trust, not money. AAA's survey found two out of three drivers do not trust repair shops in general, and 76% of those cite being sold unnecessary services. That survey is from 2016, so treat it as background, not a current measure. Still, it explains why a pushy follow-up on declined work backfires.

Sort the "no" first

Before any software sends anything, give each declined line a reason. Four reason codes are enough:

  1. Not now (money). The customer wants it done and cannot pay today.
  2. Not now (timing). Wrong week, car needed, traveling.
  3. Wants a second opinion. They are shopping the quote.
  4. No. They do not believe it is needed, or they are selling the car.

Then add one more field: how urgent is it? A seeping valve cover gasket and a brake pad at 2 mm are both "declined," but they are not the same conversation. Safety items get a different follow-up than comfort or cosmetic ones.

Your advisor enters the reason in ten seconds, when the customer says it. Software cannot guess it later.

What the software should do

  • Build a list by customer and vehicle, not a pile of old estimates. One car, its open recommendations, what was said and when.
  • Time the reminder to the reason. "Money" gets a nudge near the next payday-ish window or an offer to split the work into phases. "Timing" gets a date the customer named. "Second opinion" gets one note offering to answer questions.
  • Tie the reminder to the car. Next oil change, inspection due date, or seasonal prep is a natural reason to bring it up again, because the customer is returning anyway.
  • Stop on "No." Mark it closed and leave it closed.
  • Draft for a person. The message names the specific item and why it was recommended. The advisor approves it.
  • Show what is old. Recommendations from eight months ago may no longer match the car. The software should flag stale items so the advisor re-inspects instead of re-quoting blindly.

The message that works, and the one that does not

Compare two texts.

Bad: "Hi! You have declined services at Ridgeline Auto. Book now for a discount!"

Better: "Hi Maria, it's Dave at Ridgeline. When the Camry was in last month we noted the rear brake pads at about 3 mm. Wanted to check in before winter. Want me to hold a slot Thursday?"

The second one is specific, comes from a person, and gives the customer an easy next step. It also is not a discount. Discounting declined work teaches customers to decline. Cox Automotive's 2025 study says dissatisfied owners cite "unexpected costs and poor communication," so telling the customer what the item is and what it will cost beats a coupon.

Rules around it

If you text, the registration and consent rules from Twilio's A2P 10DLC documentation apply to your sending number, and your own lawyer should confirm what customer consent you need. Keep a record of when and why you contacted each customer. If a customer asks you to stop, stop in every channel.

Be careful with the claim itself. No shop should say it "recovers X% of declined work" without its own counted data. Count your own for 60 days: declined lines, lines revisited, lines later approved. That is your baseline.

Questions people ask

How much declined work can a shop recover?

We do not have a number we can source for independent shops. Count your own for 60 days: lines declined, lines revisited and lines later approved.

How often do customers decline recommended work?

PartsTech's 2026 survey of nearly 700 shops found 50% say customers are delaying or declining work. That measures shop perception, not an actual decline rate.

When should you follow up on declined repairs?

Tie it to the reason: a named date for timing, a phased offer for money and one offer to answer questions for second opinions. Bring up safety items first, and at the car's next visit.

Should I offer a discount on declined work?

We would not lead with one. A specific, human note about the item tends to work better, and routine discounting teaches customers to decline.
