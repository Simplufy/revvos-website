Repair status automation: tell the customer before they call

Repair status automation sends the customer a short message each time their repair order changes stage, such as inspection done, estimate ready, waiting on parts or ready for pickup. It only works when the stage on the repair order is accurate, so fix the stages first, then automate the messages and keep approvals and bad news with a person.

Updated October 25, 2026 · 6 min read

A customer drops off a pickup at 8 a.m. At noon they call to ask if it is done. At two they call again. Your advisor, who was standing next to a lift, picks up both times, and neither call moved the repair forward.

Repair status automation fixes that loop. When a repair order changes stage, the shop system or a connected tool sends the customer a short message, so they hear from you before they have to ask. This page covers which stages deserve a message, what the message should say, and the part most shops skip: making the stage on the repair order true.

What customers say they want

Customers like text. A 2022 survey of 1,000 drivers who had visited a dealership service department, run by the software vendor TEXT2DRIVE, found that about 53% want text communications and notifications. Asked which channel they prefer overall, 42% said text, 29% email and 18% phone. It is a vendor survey of dealership customers, so read it as direction, not as a measure of your shop.

Independent shops report the same pattern. Christian Brothers Automotive, a franchise group, told Repairer Driven News in 2019 that most customers say yes when asked about text notifications, and that the automated updates are generic but give customers peace of mind. Their shops still phone to confirm the full scope of work before starting.

Which stages deserve a message

Not every click in the shop system should reach the customer. Six stages cover most of what people want to know:

  1. Checked in. Confirms you have the car and the concern.
  2. Inspection done, estimate coming. Sets a time when they will hear next.
  3. Estimate ready. The only message that asks the customer to do something.
  4. Waiting on parts. Say what is late and give the new date, or say you do not have one yet.
  5. In repair. Optional. Skip it if it only adds noise.
  6. Ready for pickup. Hours, amount due and where to park.

The Christian Brothers executive quoted above said notifications go out about four times a day at their shops, which gives a sense of the ceiling. If a customer gets a message at every minor stage change, they stop reading them.

Fix the stages before you automate

Automation repeats whatever the repair order says. If a car is physically finished but the order still reads "in progress," the customer gets a wrong text, and then a call.

AutoVitals, a shop-software vendor, makes the point in a March 2026 post: unclear status causes limbo, duplicated work and late customer updates, and it recommends a clear status for every repair order with markers such as waiting for approval, parts on order and ready for pickup. Before you switch anything on, agree on three things with your team:

  • Who changes the stage, and when. The tech who finishes the work, or the advisor who closes the loop?
  • What each stage means in plain words, so "ready" never means "almost."
  • What happens when a stage is skipped or reversed. A car that goes back on the lift should not send a second "ready for pickup."

What to automate and what a person writes

Automate the plain facts: checked in, estimate sent, part ordered, ready. These are the same every time, and a template does them well.

Keep a person on anything that carries weight:

  • Approvals. An estimate needs a conversation when the number is large or the finding is a safety item.
  • Bad news. A part delayed again, a bigger repair than quoted, a car that will not be done today. Call these in.
  • Questions back. A customer who replies "why is it so much?" needs an advisor, not a second template.

Collision shops

Collision work runs longer and customers wait weeks, so updates matter more. CCC's UpdatePlus product sends automated text and email updates on repair status and a one-question survey on delivery day. A 2016 CCC report, cited in the 2019 Repairer Driven News piece above, found that keeping customers informed mattered more to satisfaction than cycle time. J.D. Power's David Pieffer made a similar point in 2018: some carriers with longer cycle times still score well when they set expectations and keep customers informed. That is an argument for honest updates, not for slower repairs.

Texting rules

If your application sends text messages over a standard 10-digit number in the US, carriers expect registration. Twilio's documentation describes A2P 10DLC as the carrier standard meant to keep application texting verified and consensual. Your texting vendor usually handles registration, but ask. Get consent when you take the work order, honor a stop request in every channel, and have your own lawyer confirm what consent you need.

How to know it is working

Count before you start. For two weeks, tally the status calls your advisors take each day, and note how many were "is my car ready." Turn automation on, then count again. We cannot give you a typical reduction, because we have no sourced figure, and we would doubt any vendor that quotes one without saying how it counted.

Questions people ask

What is repair status automation?

It is automatic messaging triggered by a change in a repair order's stage, such as estimate ready or ready for pickup. The shop system or a connected tool sends the text or email, so advisors stop answering routine status calls.

Do customers actually want text updates?

Many do. In a 2022 TEXT2DRIVE survey of 1,000 drivers, text was the preferred channel at 42%, ahead of email and phone. It was a dealership survey run by a software vendor, so test it with your own customers.

Which statuses should I send?

Start with checked in, estimate ready, waiting on parts and ready for pickup. Add inspection done if you promise a time for the estimate. Skip minor stages that only add noise.

Should the messages be written by AI?

The routine facts work as templates. Anything about price, a delay or a safety finding should come from a person, because the customer may reply and expect an answer.

Does RevvOS send customer status texts?
