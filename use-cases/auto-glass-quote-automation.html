Quoting

In auto glass, the quote is the product.

Nobody plans a windshield. It cracks, and the same afternoon the driver rings three shops. The first one that gives a complete, correct answer usually gets the job, which means the quote is not paperwork that follows the sale. It is the sale.

See it on a live quote -> The glass workflow

Anatomy

What has to be in the quote before it leaves.

A glass quote is short, and every line is load-bearing. Leave one out and the customer is ringing the next shop while you go and find it.

VIN and the exact glass
Not the year, make and model. The VIN decides which of several windshields fits, because the sensor package, the acoustic laminate, and the trim level all change the part.
OEM versus aftermarket, named
The customer is entitled to know which one they are buying. It also changes what a carrier will pay, so the quote has to say which part it is priced on rather than leaving the question open.
The small parts
Sensor pads, clip kits, molding, urethane, and the primer that goes with it. These carry their own numbers and their own cost, and they are the line items most often missing from a hand-written glass quote.
Recalibration, stated either way
A modern windshield usually carries a recalibration requirement for the camera behind it. The quote has to say whether calibration is included and what it costs. Silence here becomes an argument at pickup.
Mobile or in-shop, and when
The two paths carry different prices and different promises. The customer wants to know whether the van comes to them and how long the car is out of service either way.
Both payment paths
The insurance path and the cash path in the same message: what the carrier is expected to cover, what the glass deductible leaves with the customer, and what the total is if they simply pay. Most callers do not know which path they are on until they see both.
Tax, fees, and the expiry
The number the customer hears has to be the number they pay. A quote with a surprise at the counter is a quote that loses to whichever shop was clearer.

Why speed decides it

The call you are on is a bidding war you cannot see.

Three shops, one afternoon, and the customer is comparing on two axes only: how fast somebody answered, and whether the answer sounded complete. A glass quote is not a considered purchase, so the shop that needs a callback to price it is usually the shop that loses it.

T+0

The call comes in

The driver is in a parking lot with a crack spreading across the passenger side, and they are ringing whoever they can find first. They have no idea what it should cost.

T+2 min

They are still on the line

This is the only window where the shop owns the customer outright. Once they hang up to "call you back with a price", they are somebody else's lead.

T+1 hr

The other shop answered

By now the driver has a number and a time from someone else. Coming back with a better price later is possible, and it is a much harder conversation.

T+1 day

The job is booked elsewhere

The work went to whoever answered completely and quickly. The follow-up question is not how to win the job back, it is why the quote took so long.

What automation does

Four jobs, none of them pricing.

01

Assembles the quote from the VIN and the part data

The decode, the candidate part, the small parts that belong with it, and the recalibration flag, gathered in one pass while the caller is still talking.

02

Shows the insurance path and the cash path together

Both numbers in one message, with the deductible and the recalibration cost broken out, so the caller can choose instead of calling back to ask.

03

Captures the customer while they are still on the phone

Name, vehicle, VIN, address, carrier, and an accepted appointment slot if the shop has the availability. The record is complete before the call ends.

04

Follows up a quote that did not close

The next morning, with the same part and the same number in hand, so the message is a reminder rather than a new sales pitch. How glass lead follow-up works ->

The honest limits

Three things the shop still owns, permanently.

Your pricing

Automation does not set a price, does not discount, and does not decide what a job is worth. It repeats the numbers the shop has already decided.

Your part sourcing

Which supplier, which brand, and whether aftermarket glass is good enough for a given vehicle are judgment calls the shop makes and stands behind.

Your insurance terms

Participation agreements, network rules, and what the shop has agreed to bill are between the shop and the carrier. Automation does not negotiate them and does not promise what a carrier will pay.

One more line worth drawing: automation does not decide whether a chip can be repaired or whether a windshield needs replacing. It can quote the replacement a technician has already called for. It cannot make that call.
