Detailing

A ceramic coating lead is a considered purchase wearing a detail lead's clothes.

It arrives looking like an ordinary booking request. It behaves like a six-figure decision with a five-year horizon, taken by someone who has read three forum threads and watched forty before-and-after clips, and who is not going to decide on the first phone call.

See it on your pipeline -> Detailing overview

Lead Physics

One of these two leads needs a decision. The other needs a belief.

Shops that run a repair-style follow-up on a coating lead usually conclude the lead was cold. It was not cold. It was unconvinced, which is a different problem with a different fix.

A repair lead

Has something broken or due, and the vehicle decides the urgency.

Compares by price and availability, usually across a handful of shops.

The decision window is days, sometimes hours.

Says yes to a quote. The work is a known quantity.

Follow-up is a nudge: still want to book it?

A ceramic coating lead

Has a vehicle that is working fine. Nothing forces the decision except the owner's own timeline.

Compares by confidence: is this product real, will this shop prep it properly, will it look like the photos.

The decision window runs weeks, and it moves with the weather and with the owner's schedule.

Says yes to an inspection. The work depends on what the paint turns out to be.

Follow-up is education: here is what you are actually buying and why the prep is the product.

Origins

Five places a coating lead comes from, and five different follow-ups.

Where the lead came from determines what it already knows. Treating all five the same is how a shop sends a correction pitch to someone who was never asking about correction.

UPSELL

The existing detail customer

Already trusts the shop and knows the work. Needs the value case for the upgrade, not a reintroduction.

CONTENT

Social and before-and-after

Came in through a video or a post on social media. Responds to visuals and to specific vehicles, because that is the content they already engaged with.

REFERRAL

A friend's car

Arrives pre-sold on quality and usually pre-sold on price. Shortest path to an inspection, and the easiest to lose by being slow.

WALK-IN

Someone at the counter

The car may be outside. This is the one lead that can be inspected on the spot, which makes it the most likely to close today.

INBOUND

A new enquiry from search or ads

Knows the least and is shopping hardest. Everything it knows about the shop it learned in the last ten minutes.

The Stall

"Goes dark" is not a reason. It is a symptom with four causes.

A coating lead that stops responding has usually stalled on one of four things, and the follow-up that unsticks it is different in each case. Sending the same "just checking in" message four times is how a shop trains a lead to ignore it.

Comparing packages

Two entry coatings and a multi-year coating look identical in a quote. The buyer is trying to work out what the extra money buys, and the answer is durability and prep, not branding.

Unsure of the value

The car already looks fine. Nobody has explained what the coating is protecting against, or what changes day to day when it is on.

Worried about prep

The lead has read enough to know that a coating over uncorrected paint locks in the defects. That worry is the single strongest signal a shop can get, because it means the lead understands the product.

Waiting on correction first

The lead is not delaying the coating. The lead is deciding whether to pay for correction, which is the larger line on the ticket and the one that determines what the coating even means.

Two clocks are running at once: the lead's comparison, which can run for a month, and the shop's bay, which does not have a month. A follow-up cadence is what keeps the second clock from running the lead out of the shop by accident.

The Cadence

Five touches, each one adding something the last one did not.

The rule that makes this work: no touch repeats the previous one. Every message either teaches, shows, or asks, and the ask gets smaller each time until it is the smallest possible yes.

SAME DAY

Answer the actual question, in writing

The reply goes back with the vehicle, the package tiers, and what the prep involves. Nobody has inspected the paint yet, so there is no price on correction and the message says so plainly.

DAY 2

Show the prep, not the shine

A shot of the decontamination and the panel wipe on a car like theirs does more than any finish photo, because prep is the thing the lead is quietly worried about. This is the touch that separates a shop from a competitor sending a discount.

DAY 4 TO 7 / THE ASK

Offer the inspection, not the package

Bring the car in, put a light on the paint, show the owner what correction it needs, and price it from there. No commitment to a coating package, which is the whole reason this ask is easy to accept.

AFTER THE INSPECTION

Put the findings and the options in one place

What the paint measured, what correction it needs, which package fits how long they plan to keep the car, and what the two together cost. A considered buyer needs something to take away and read twice.

WEEK 3 AND BEYOND

Stay in the season, then stop

A coating decision lands when the car is about to face winter salt, a long drive, or a fresh purchase. Those are real reasons to check back, and they are finite. A cadence with no end trains a list to ignore the shop.

The Honest Structure Of The Sale

The first conversion is an inspection, and it has to be.

Nobody can price a coating job over the phone, because the price is not the coating. The price is the correction the paint needs before the coating goes on, and that is decided by scratch depth, defect type, and how much clear coat is left to work with. All three require the vehicle.

That has a direct consequence for how a lead is scored. A shop that measures success by booked coating packages will read its own pipeline wrong, because the real conversion is the inspection booking. Until the paint has been read under a light, the lead is not a coating sale. It is a maybe with a date on it.

Stated the other way: every follow-up on this page is aimed at getting the car in front of someone who owns a paint gauge and a light, and nothing else. That is a much smaller ask than a four-figure commitment, and it is the only ask that produces a defensible number.

What the automation owns

Capturing the vehicle, the package tier, and the correction question on first contact.

Keeping the cadence moving on the right day, for every lead, without an advisor remembering to.

Sending the education and the before-and-after content at the point it is relevant.

Reporting the inspection booking rate and where leads go quiet.

What stays human

Reading the paint and pricing the correction.

The conversation about what the coating will and will not do on that specific vehicle.

Deciding whether the job is a fit for the shop's bay time and capabilities.

Boundary

Two things no coating follow-up should ever say.

A durability number the shop has not verified on that vehicle.

Coating life depends on prep, climate, wash habits, and how the car is stored. The number on the bottle is not a promise the shop can make about somebody's driveway.

A result the shop has not seen.

Before-and-after content from a similar car is evidence. A claim about this car, before anyone has looked at it, is a liability with a delivery date attached.
