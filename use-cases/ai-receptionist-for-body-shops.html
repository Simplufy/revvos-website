Collision Front Desk

An AI receptionist for body shops answers a different phone.

A mechanical shop's phone rings with a noise complaint and a time request. A collision shop's phone rings with a person standing next to a damaged car, an adjuster with a question about a supplement, or a customer on day nine of a rental who wants to know one specific thing. Same technology, completely different call set. The call set has to come first.

The Call Set

Seven calls a collision shop gets, and what each one actually requires.

Each row below is a real call type with a different required outcome. Two columns matter: what the receptionist must capture before the call ends, and what has to reach a person while the caller is still on the line. Getting the second column wrong loses a shop a customer.

CALL 01

A new accident

HIGHEST STAKES
Must capture

Is the car drivable, is anyone hurt, where is the car, who else is involved, has the insurer been notified, and a callback number that works. Drivability, injury and location decide everything, in that order.

Route immediately

Any mention of an injury, any question about fault, and any request for a tow the shop does not control. A tow is a commitment about time and money, so a person takes it.

CALL 02

A DRP or insurer referral

ROUTING SENSITIVE
Must capture

Which insurer, the claim number if one exists, whether the caller is the policyholder or the insurer, the referring adjuster's name, and whether the car is being directed here specifically. "My insurance told me to call you" and "my insurance is sending me to you" are different intakes.

Route immediately

Any call where the insurer is asking the shop to accept or decline a job, and any referral outside normal intake hours. Accepting a DRP job is a manager's decision.

CALL 03

A status call from a customer in a rental

HIGHEST VOLUME
Must capture

The vehicle, so the answer is about the right car, and what the caller actually wants: whether the part arrived, whether the insurer approved the supplement, whether it will be done Friday. Three different questions, and a generic answer satisfies none.

Route immediately

Any caller on their second or third call about the same car, and any call where the shop's data shows the repair has passed the promised date. Do not let a system absorb frustration.

CALL 04

An adjuster or appraiser call

NEVER AUTONOMOUS
Must capture

Who is calling, which claim, which vehicle, and what they want: a re-inspection, a supplement question, a parts dispute, or a total-loss valuation. They need a specific person, so the routing target matters more than the message.

Route immediately

All of them. An adjuster call is never a message-taking job. The receptionist identifies the claim and the need, then puts a person on.

CALL 05

A parts or sublet vendor call

CAPTURE AND LOG
Must capture

Which shop, which job or estimate number, which part or sublet, and the message: an ETA, a price change, a substitution, or a no-show. A glass vendor calling with a date is one of the highest-value calls a collision shop gets, and it usually arrives at the counter, where nobody writes it down.

Route immediately

A vendor calling with a problem: a part that arrived damaged, a sublet that cannot be honored, a price change against an approved estimate. Those change the plan.

CALL 06

A total-loss or diminished-value question

ROUTE ON SIGHT
Must capture

Whether the car is in the shop or the question is arriving cold, the claim number, and whether the customer has already been told the vehicle is a total loss. The job here is to identify the call type, not to discuss numbers.

Route immediately

Always, and to a person with authority. A total-loss or diminished-value conversation is the most sensitive call a collision shop takes, with a customer who is having a bad month.

CALL 07

A tow arrival

CLOCK STARTS HERE
Must capture

Which car is arriving, on which truck, at what time, and whether anyone at the shop is expecting it. This call is short, and it creates the intake record: the VIN, the location on the lot, and the fact that a vehicle is now the shop's responsibility.

Route immediately

A car nobody has a record of, or a driver reporting damage that was not on the ticket. Both mean the shop needs eyes on the vehicle today.

Escalation Rules

Three triggers that pull a person onto the line, regardless of what was asked.

A call set tells you what to capture. Escalation rules tell you when capturing is the wrong move.

Trigger 01

Anything about fault or legal

Who was at fault, whether someone should call a lawyer, what the police report says, whether the other driver's insurer will accept liability. The receptionist captures the facts as stated and transfers. It does not comment, agree, or offer an opinion. A shop's words on fault can end up in a claim file.

Trigger 02

An angry customer whose repair ran long

Recognized by language about time lost, rental cost, or a promise someone made. The receptionist cannot fix this and should not try. It apologizes for the hold, confirms the vehicle and the callback number, and hands the call to a person before the customer repeats themselves. The worst outcome here is a confident answer that turns out to be wrong.

Trigger 03

An insurer negotiation

A call where an insurer is asking the shop to change, justify, or accept something on the repair: a labor rate, a parts choice, a supplement line, a total-loss threshold. These are negotiation calls. No automated system should be in the position of agreeing to anything; the receptionist's only job is to identify the claim and route it to whoever owns that relationship.

Hard Limits

What an AI receptionist at a body shop must never do.

These are not configuration preferences. They are why a body shop can run one of these without putting its insurer relationships at risk.

Quote a repair

No dollar figure for a repair, a paint job, a part, or a sublet. No range, no ballpark, no what-someone-paid-last-time.

A collision estimate is a document with a lifecycle, and it changes after teardown. A number quoted on the phone is a number that will move.

Promise a completion date

No commitment on when a car will be finished, ready for pickup, or out of paint. Not "probably Friday".

A date given on the phone becomes the promise the customer holds the shop to, and in collision that date depends on parts, supplements and sublets not settled at intake.

Give insurance or legal guidance

No advice on whether to file a claim, whether to accept a settlement, whether a vehicle will be declared a total loss, or what a policy covers.

These answers come from the insurer or the customer's own advisor, never from the shop's front desk.

Everything the receptionist does sits on the other side of that line: it answers, captures, books a slot for a person to confirm, attaches a message to the right car, and routes. The estimate, the supplement, the appraiser conversation and the total-loss discussion stay with your people.
