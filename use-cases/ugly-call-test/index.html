The Ugly Call Test: a one-week AI phone answering trial for auto repair shops

An AI phone answering trial should run for one normal week in your own shop, on your own messy calls, before you sign anything. The Ugly Call Test gives you the plan: log every call, make your own ugly test calls on a bad line and by asking for someone by name, write a never list of things it may not do alone, and judge it on its handoffs as much as its bookings.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

This is the kit from our video on AI phone answering for auto repair shops. It follows the video:

  1. Log every call for one normal week.
  2. Make the ugly calls yourself: a bad line, a mumbled make, asking for someone by name.
  3. Write the never list before the week starts: what it may never do alone.
  4. Watch the handoffs, not only the bookings. A transfer to nobody is worse than a ring.

Every vendor demo is a smooth call. Your Tuesday afternoon isn't, so that's what you test.

Why test it at all

Calls go unanswered because the people who'd answer them are busy with customers. People don't pick up unknown numbers either: Hiya's report says 86% of calls from unknown numbers go unanswered. Shop software vendors have built answering tools for this. AutoLeap's AIR books into the AutoLeap calendar and lists per-call and monthly plans, and Shopmonkey's Concierge takes calls after hours, captures the customer and vehicle, and can transfer or create follow-up tasks. If your shop already runs one of those systems, ask them first. Whichever tool you look at, the test below is the same.

Before the week (about an hour)

Pick a week with a normal mix of work, not a holiday week. Then write down three things.

Your five most common calls. Most shops hear some version of these: an appointment request, "what's the status of my car," a price on a job, "is it done," and a parts or vendor call.

The never list. These are things the system must never do on its own. Start with these three and add your own:

  • Quote a price for work nobody has inspected.
  • Promise a pickup time.
  • Tell a customer a car is safe to drive.

The handoff plan. Who gets a transferred call, and who's the backup when that person is under a car. Write the hours each one covers.

During the week: the call log

Keep one line per call. A spreadsheet or a clipboard both work.

Time What the caller wanted What the system did Did a person have to fix it?
8:12 (example) appointment, brakes booked Thursday 9:00 no
9:40 (example) is my car done handed to the advisor, notes attached no
2:15 (example) asked for Mike transferred, nobody answered yes

Ask your front-desk person to mark every call where they thought "I'd have said that differently." Those marks are often more useful than the vendor's dashboard.

The ugly calls to make yourself

Call your own number six or seven times during the week, from different phones. Script them loosely:

  1. The bad line. Call from somewhere with one bar of signal, or with traffic noise behind you.
  2. The mumbled make. Say "it's the, uh, Silverado, maybe a 2016" and see what it captures.
  3. The name. Ask for a specific person by first name, the way regulars do.
  4. The noisy engine. Describe a sound with no vehicle details and see if it asks the right questions.
  5. The price. Ask what a brake job costs. It should not quote uninspected work.
  6. The safety question. Ask whether it's safe to keep driving. It should hand that to a person.
  7. The angry caller. Be short and annoyed about a delay. It should get you to a person fast.

Log each one with a "TEST" tag so you can separate them from real calls.

At the end of the week: count four things

  1. Real bookings. Appointments that are actually on your calendar, not just "booked" in the vendor's report.
  2. Handoffs. Calls passed to a person, and how many arrived with the right notes.
  3. Visible mistakes. Calls it got wrong in a way a customer would notice.
  4. Dead ends. Calls where it did nothing useful and the caller hung up.

You'd expect bookings to be the number that decides it. Watch the handoffs just as closely. A caller who gets transferred to nobody has a worse experience than one who simply hears the phone ring, and that's the call that turns into a review. If you can't get these four numbers from the vendor's own reporting, that tells you something about the vendor.

Questions for the demo call

  • Where does the booking land? In your shop system, or a separate calendar someone copies over? Tekmetric lists 70+ integrations, so ask about your specific system, not "do you integrate."
  • What happens when it doesn't know? The right answer is a handoff with the notes captured so far.
  • Can you hear recordings and read transcripts? Ask for both.
  • What counts as a call for billing? Get the per-call and monthly numbers in writing.
  • Who owns the phone number? You should be able to leave and keep it.

Rules to check before go-live

Recording a call can require consent from everyone on it. Rev's state-by-state guide lists California, Florida, Illinois and Pennsylvania among the all-party consent states, so if you record, the greeting should say so. Have your lawyer confirm your state's rule.

For inbound calls, the FCC has said the TCPA's requirements don't extend to technologies used to answer inbound calls. Outbound is different. The FCC ruled in 2024 that AI-generated voices count as artificial under the TCPA, so if the same tool calls customers back, ask how it handles consent.

Questions people ask

How long should an AI phone answering trial last?

One normal week, with a typical mix of work. That's enough to see real calls, make your own test calls and count bookings, handoffs, mistakes and dead ends.

What should an AI receptionist never do on its own at an auto shop?

Quote a price for work nobody has inspected, promise a pickup time, or tell a customer a car is safe to drive. Those go to a person.

What test calls should I make during the trial?

Call from a bad line, mumble the vehicle details, ask for someone by name, describe a noise with no details, ask for a price and a safety opinion, and play an annoyed customer.

Do I need consent to record AI-answered calls?

In all-party consent states, everyone on the call has to consent to recording, so the greeting should say the call may be recorded. Check your state's rule with your lawyer.
