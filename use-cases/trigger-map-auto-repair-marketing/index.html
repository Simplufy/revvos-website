The Trigger Map: four auto repair follow-up messages and who checks each one

The Trigger Map sets up auto repair follow-up messages as four rows, each with one shop event, one message you approved ahead of time and one person who checks it: the booked appointment, tomorrow's visit, the closed repair order and the declined job. Copy the table, the four message templates, the consent basics and the first-month setup below.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

The Trigger Map is a one-page plan for a repair shop's automated messages: every row is one event in your shop system, one message you approved ahead of time and one person who checks it. You only need the same four rows as the video to start:

  1. The booked appointment: a confirmation the moment the visit is on the schedule, checked by the front desk once at setup.
  2. Tomorrow's visit: a short reminder the day before, checked by the same front desk.
  3. The closed repair order: a "how's it running?" message a few days later, with the service manager reading a sample every month.
  4. The declined job: a reminder that links to the estimate the advisor wrote, checked by that advisor, because only they know why it was declined.

Below is the full map, the message wording for each row, the consent basics and a first-month setup you can copy.

Why triggers beat campaigns

Marketing automation sounds like big campaigns. In a shop it's simpler: something happens in your shop system, and a message you approved goes out. Tekmetric's automated reminders page describes exactly that pattern: confirmations when a visit is booked, pre-visit reminders, post-visit follow-ups once the repair order closes, and service reminders for declined jobs and upcoming maintenance.

Plenty of shops haven't started. In the 2026 Ratchet+Wrench Industry Survey, 15% of respondents who use AI tools said they use them for marketing automation. The shops that have started usually began with the rows below, because they go to people who already know the shop. Our longer page on auto repair marketing automation covers the wider picture.

Think of the map like the warning lights on a dashboard: each light comes on for one specific reason, and you know exactly who looks at it. A message with no event behind it, or no person checking it, is the one that ends up sounding robotic.

Who it is for

  • Independent shops whose shop management system can send texts or emails but who never set the rules.
  • Service managers who want automated messages without losing control of the wording.
  • Multi-shop groups that want the same four rows running the same way at every location.

What you need first

  • A shop management system that can fire a message on an event (booked, day before, repair order closed, job declined). Check what yours can do before writing anything.
  • The names of the people who will check each row.
  • Consent records for texting, from your system or your vendor.
  • An hour to write four messages in your own voice.

The four rows, with messages to copy

The names and details below are demo examples. Replace them with your shop's.

Event Message Checked by How often it's checked
Appointment booked Confirmation Front desk Once, at setup
Visit tomorrow Reminder Front desk Once, at setup
Repair order closed "How's it running?" Service manager A sample every month
Job declined Link to the advisor's estimate The advisor who wrote it Every message

Row 1. The booked appointment

Hi Sam, you're booked at Redline Auto Route 9 on Tuesday at 8:30 AM. Reply here if you need to change the time. (demo)

Keep it to the facts: day, time, location and how to change it. No promotions in a service message.

Row 2. Tomorrow's visit

See you tomorrow at 8:30, Sam. If something came up, reply and we'll find another time. (demo)

One reminder is enough. If your system can send two, decide that on purpose and cap it.

Row 3. The closed repair order

Hi Sam, it's Redline Auto. How's the Camry running since Friday? If anything feels off, reply here and Luis will call you. (demo)

Send it a few days after the visit, not the same hour. Have the service manager read a handful of replies each month, because the answers tell you about the work, not just the message.

Row 4. The declined job

Hi Sam, here's the rear brake estimate Priya wrote for you: (estimate link). If you have questions about it, reply and she'll get back to you. (demo)

This is the row where shops go wrong. You'd think the reminder should repeat the price and push. Link to the estimate instead, so the number the customer sees is the one the advisor wrote, and let the advisor check the message. Only the advisor knows whether the customer was waiting on payday, getting a second opinion or just wasn't convinced the work was needed. Our page on declined work follow-up goes deeper.

Consent and the legal basics

Email is the simpler case. The CAN-SPAM Act requires a commercial email to identify itself as an advertisement (unless the person gave prior affirmative consent), include a valid physical postal address and give a clear way to opt out. Once someone asks to stop, you have 10 business days at most. Keep service messages and promotions separate so it's obvious which rules apply.

Texting has its own rules, including consent and carrier registration for business texts sent from an app. Our page on AI text messaging for auto repair covers that. Ask your vendor what consent it records, and ask a lawyer about anything beyond that.

What to keep human

  • Prices and promises. Link to the estimate the advisor wrote rather than restating a number.
  • Unhappy customers. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 89% of consumers expect owners to respond to reviews. If a "how's it running?" reply is a complaint, a person answers it.
  • How many messages one customer gets in a month. Set a cap.
  • Anything that states a fact about your shop: hours, warranty terms, certifications. Check your Google listing too, because Google says complete and accurate info is more likely to show, and a reminder that sends someone to the wrong hours undoes the work.

A first-month setup

  1. Write the four rows on one page and put a name in every "Checked by" cell.
  2. Write one plain message per row in your own voice.
  3. Turn on rows 1 and 2 first. Read every message they send for the first week.
  4. Turn on rows 3 and 4 in week two. Read the first twenty messages yourself before you trust them.
  5. Set the monthly cap per customer.

How to tell it worked

After a month, count your own numbers: appointments confirmed or moved by reply, "how's it running?" replies that flagged a problem, declined estimates that came back as booked work, and opt-outs or complaints. We don't cite an industry benchmark, because none we can verify fits your shop. Questions people ask

What automated messages should an auto repair shop start with?

Start with four: a confirmation when an appointment is booked, a reminder the day before, a check-in a few days after the repair order closes, and a reminder for declined work that links to the advisor's estimate.

Should a declined work reminder include the price?

Link to the estimate the advisor wrote instead of restating a number, and let that advisor check the message, because they know why the customer said no.

How many reminders is too many?

Decide a monthly cap per customer and stick to it. One confirmation and one reminder per visit is a sensible start.

Do shop texts need consent?

Yes, texting has its own consent and registration rules. Ask your shop management vendor what consent it records and ask a lawyer about anything unclear.
