Paint protection film

A paint protection film shop does not sell jobs. It sells panels.

That distinction is the whole reason generic shop software fits a PPF operation badly. A repair ticket has one line item and one price. A film job is a coverage decision across a set of panels, each with its own cut, its own corners, and its own add-on value later.

See it on your workflow -> Detailing overview

The Unit Of Work

Four coverage tiers, and the panels that define them.

Almost every PPF conversation resolves into one of four coverage tiers. The tiers are what the customer is actually buying, and the panels inside them are what the shop actually installs. A quote that does not name both is a quote that will be argued about later.

Tier 01

Partial front

The high-impact strip. Usually the front bumper, a portion of the hood, and the mirror caps.

Entry point. Smallest bay time, fastest add-on conversation.
Tier 02

Full front

The full front clip: bumper, entire hood, both front fenders, mirrors, headlights, and typically the A-pillars.

The most common retail tier and the most quoted.
Tier 03

Front plus rockers

Full front with the lower rocker panels, door cups, door edges, and often the rear bumper load area.

Usually a daily driver or a truck that sees gravel and job sites.
Tier 04

Full body

Every painted panel including roof, doors, rear quarters, and trunk or hatch.

Multi-day bay time. The job that most needs its schedule protected.

Two shoppers, one price. A partial front on a sedan and a full front on the same sedan are different jobs with different material and different bay time. This is where PPF quotes go wrong first, both in the shop and between shops.

The Quote Record

Six fields that have to be identical every single time.

PPF quoting fails quietly. Nobody makes a dramatic error. What happens is that the same car gets quoted twice, weeks apart, with a different tier or a different film line, and the shop discovers it when the customer brings the second quote in.

The fix is not a better quote template. It is a quote record whose fields are mandatory rather than optional, so a partial front and a full front can never be confused in a hurry and a film line can never be assumed.

Panels

Named individually, not implied by the tier. "Full front" is shorthand, not a specification.

Coverage tier

Which of the four this job sits in, so the shop's own history is comparable across vehicles.

Pre-cut or bulk

Plotter-cut patterns and bulk hand-cut both have a place, and they carry different labor. The record should say which, because the customer will ask why an add-on later costs what it costs.

Film line and lot

The exact product, and the lot it came from. This field exists for the warranty claim, not for the invoice.

Edge treatment

Wrapped or trimmed, and where. Edge behavior is the most common comeback on a film job.

Paint condition at install

Noted before the film goes on. Film preserves paint, so what the paint looked like at install is the baseline for every later conversation.

Eight stages, and the two where the craft lives.

Read this as the film job moving left to right. Automation is useful across most of it and has no business at all in the middle.

Inquiry and vehicle capture

Coverage decision

Pre-install paint inspection

Under a light, on the actual panels. This decides whether correction happens first and it decides whether the film goes on at all today.

Scheduling around the bay

Install and film cure

The craft. Edges, relief cuts, stretch, and the settle period after, when the customer has to leave it alone and the shop has to be reachable if something lifts.

Film and warranty record

Which film lot went on which panel of which vehicle, on what date. Without this, a warranty conversation starts from a guess.

Care guidance and panel add-ons

How to wash it, what not to do, and the later conversation about adding the panels they skipped the first time.

What It Actually Fixes

Four failures a PPF shop will recognize.

None of these are dramatic. All four cost money quietly, and all four get worse as the shop takes on more film work.

01

The same car quoted two different ways

Weeks apart, by two different people, with a different tier in mind. The customer notices before the shop does, and the shop looks like it makes numbers up.

02

A warranty claim with no film record

A panel lifts eighteen months later and nobody can say which lot went on it or when. The claim becomes a negotiation the shop is not positioned to win.

03

The customer who wants to add panels

They took the partial front, drove it six months, and now want the hood and rockers. Whether that is easy depends entirely on whether the first install was recorded well enough to match film and layout.

04

The before-and-after nobody kept

PPF sells on before-and-after, and the best evidence a shop will ever have is the car currently in the bay, photographed at the right two moments. If nothing prompts those shots, the shop is left buying stock imagery.

Boundary

Automation does not decide coverage, and it does not install film.

Both of those are judgment and craft, and both belong to the shop. Software can hold the tier definitions steady, make sure the six quote fields are never skipped, keep the film record, and put the follow-up in front of someone on the right day. It cannot look at a bumper and decide that a partial front is the wrong call for a car that commutes on a gravel road, and it certainly cannot lay a stretch over a compound curve without a lift or a bubble.

Stated as a rule: the system prepares the decision and protects the record. The installer and the person who inspects the paint make the calls that a customer will remember.
