Auto glass use case

Auto glass dispatch automation: the route is the operation.

Search this phrase and you get dispatch built for insurance networks and telematics partnerships that put a glass technician on a connected fleet. Useful for the enterprise side. Not much help to an independent glass shop that has to build tomorrow from scratch, because glass is a mobile business where the daily schedule is the product, not an afterthought bolted onto the shop floor.

Walk through a glass day -> The auto glass pillar

The four units

A glass day is four things moving at once.

Mechanical dispatch moves one resource and one vehicle. Mobile glass dispatch moves four things that can each ruin the day independently. Any tool that only sees one of them is guessing about the other three.

Unit 01

The technician

One person, one skill set, one set of certifications. A tech who can set a windshield but is not cleared for a static ADAS calibration is a different dispatch unit than one who is.

Unit 02

The van and its shelf

A van is a small warehouse with a fixed capacity. What is on the shelf right now determines which jobs are even possible today, before anyone talks about distance.

Unit 03

The glass part

A specific part for a specific vehicle. VIN-decoded fitment, a NAGS number, and a decision about OEM versus aftermarket that the customer or the insurer may have a say in. It is not interchangeable with a similar-looking windshield.

Unit 04

The route

Not a straight line between addresses. It is whatever sequence of stops actually fits inside the working hours once cure times and calibration stops are subtracted, and it changes the moment a same-day call comes in.

The constraints

Four things that make glass dispatch different.

None of these are exotic. Every glass shop lives with all four every day, which is exactly why they get handled by memory instead of by a system.

01

The exact part has to be on the van

A close-enough part is not a part. If the windshield on the shelf does not match the VIN, the technician arrives, cannot do the job, and the customer has lost a morning. That is worse than a rescheduled appointment, because the customer already arranged their day around it.

02

Cure time sets a floor the route has to respect

Urethane cure time and drive-away time mean the vehicle is not finished when the glass is set. The tech is done, the car is not. A schedule that treats the install as the end of the stop quietly books the next job into a gap that does not exist.

03

A calibration can turn a mobile job into an in-shop one

If the vehicle needs a static calibration, the work may have to come to the shop, and that single fact can move a job across the county. Dynamic calibrations behave differently again. Dispatch has to know which one applies before the route is set, not after the tech is already forty minutes out.

04

The customer often cannot wait

A cracked windshield is not a next-Tuesday problem for a lot of drivers, and a fleet vehicle out of service is a cost that starts immediately. The best route is often the one that absorbs the call that arrived twenty minutes ago, which means the day has to stay rebuildable.

The build order

How a glass day actually gets built.

There is a real order to it. Shops that dispatch well tend to follow the same sequence without calling it a process.

First

Geography sets the shape, job type refines it

The route starts as a cluster of addresses. Route density is the first number that matters, because two jobs twelve minutes apart are worth more than two jobs forty minutes apart at the same ticket. Then job type sorts the cluster: a windshield replacement and a chip repair are not the same length of stop, and a mobile replacement is not the same stop as one that has to come in for calibration.

Then

Hold the right parts before promising the stop

A job is only dispatchable if the part exists for it: on the van, in the shop, or on a transfer that will land in time. This is the step shops skip under pressure, and it is the step that produces the worst customer experience in the business.

Then

Sequence the calibrations

Static calibrations get grouped at the shop because they cannot travel. Dynamic ones can be worked into the road day, but they need open road and daylight. Treating the two the same is how a route that looked efficient in the morning falls apart by two in the afternoon.

Always

Leave room for the same-day chip or crack

The emergency call is not an interruption to a glass business, it is part of the demand pattern. A day built with zero slack will not absorb it, and the shop ends up choosing between a stranded customer and a promise it cannot keep.

The honest split

What the layer prepares, and what stays with a person.

Dispatch is a good candidate for automation precisely because the parts that are hard are arithmetic and memory, not judgment. The judgment stays where it belongs.

The layer can prepare
  • Route options that respect cure and drive-away time instead of treating each stop as finished when the glass is set.
  • A flag on any job whose part is not staged, so the promise is not made before the part is real.
  • A signal when a technician is carrying more than the day can hold, or sitting idle while a call is waiting.
  • A match between an incoming job and the actual remaining capacity on the board, not the theoretical capacity.
  • A rebuilt version of the day when a same-day call arrives, offered for review rather than applied.
A person still owns
  • The call on the damage itself: whether a windshield can be repaired, whether it must be replaced, and what that means for the vehicle.
  • Every representation to an insurer or a TPA. The layer can assemble and track; it does not negotiate and it does not speak for the shop.
  • The technician's own read of a job site: weather, access, a customer who wants to watch, a driveway that will not take the van.
  • The decision to move a customer onto or off a promise, which is a commitment a person makes and a person owns.

The line that does not move: scheduling software can decide which technician is closest. It cannot decide that a windshield is safe, or that a vehicle is safe to drive away. Those determinations belong to qualified people, and any system that implies otherwise is selling something it cannot deliver.
