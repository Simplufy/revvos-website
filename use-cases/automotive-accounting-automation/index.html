Automotive accounting automation: what to automate and what to keep

Automotive accounting automation is software that moves sales, payments and parts purchases from your shop management system to your books and flags where the two disagree, so nobody retypes tickets. A person still maps the accounts, reviews exceptions, closes the month and answers for the tax treatment.

Updated October 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Most shops already have two sets of numbers. One lives in the shop management system, where the service advisor closes repair orders. The other lives in the books, where a bookkeeper or the owner records sales, parts bills and payroll. Automotive accounting automation is the work of getting those two to agree without someone retyping every ticket on a Sunday night.

Where the double entry happens

A repair order does a lot of jobs. When the work is done it becomes the invoice, and Tekmetric describes the RO turning into an invoice once the job is complete. Payment is taken at the counter, by text or through a payment link. Parts are bought from a few vendors. Then someone has to show all of that in the accounting file.

That is where the same facts get entered twice. Sales, payments, parts purchases, refunds and cash drawer counts all exist in the shop system and again in QuickBooks or whatever the books run on. Every manual entry is a chance for a typo, a missed ticket or a purchase posted to the wrong month.

What can be automated today

Three things are well understood and widely available:

  1. Sending sales, payments and purchases to the books. Tekmetric, for example, integrates with The Back Office, whose Accounting Link tool sends sales, payments and purchases to QuickBooks. Users can review the data before it is sent, which avoids re-entry and typing errors. Other shop systems have their own accounting connections, so check which one yours supports and what it covers.
  2. Matching parts bought to parts sold. Both systems hold parts data. Comparing the shop system's parts purchased and parts usage reports against the vendor-sorted cost of goods report in the books shows where they drift apart.
  3. Reminders and exceptions. A tool can notice a ticket that is closed but unpaid, or a vendor bill with no matching purchase order, and put it in front of a person.

The first is a built-in feature of many shop systems. The second and third are where an AI agent adds something, because they are checking work, not moving a number from A to B.

What the mismatches look like

Tekmetric's own accounting guide gives examples worth copying, because they show how small gaps hide. In its example, the shop system showed 25,000 dollars of parts cost while QuickBooks showed 35,000 dollars, a 10,000 dollar gap. In another, a repeat part showed 2 orders in the shop system and 10 in the books, and 100 oil filters were bought with none used in two months. Those are the article's illustrations, not industry averages.

The guide's advice is plain: check numbers monthly, reconcile thoroughly each quarter, and match every paid part to a paid ticket with regular spot checks. It also notes the shop system shows what you think you earned, while accounting software captures costs the shop system typically misses, such as paid time off, benefits and payroll taxes.

A practical setup

If you are starting from spreadsheets and re-typing, this is a sensible order:

  1. Decide who owns the books: you, a bookkeeper or a CPA. The SBA suggests considering a certified public accountant, a bookkeeper or an online service for the work.
  2. Turn on the accounting connection your shop system supports, and map your chart of accounts once, with your bookkeeper, before any data moves.
  3. Review before posting. Keep the step where a person looks at what is about to be sent.
  4. Reconcile on a schedule. Monthly for parts, quarterly for the whole file, as the guide above suggests.
  5. Write down what the automation does not handle. Voids, refunds, cores and sublet work are the usual gaps, so ask your integration vendor how each one flows.

What to keep with a person

  • The chart of accounts and the mapping. A wrong mapping is wrong at scale.
  • Month-end close and sign-off. Your bookkeeper or CPA stands behind the statements, not the software.
  • Tax treatment. The IRS says you may choose any recordkeeping system suited to your business that clearly shows your income and expenses, and that you must keep records as long as needed to prove income or deductions on a return. Automation does not change who is responsible for that.
  • Cash versus accrual. The SBA explains that the accrual method records a sale when it is complete, while the cash method records it once payment is received. Pick with your accountant, then make sure the automation follows it.

Questions people ask

What is automotive accounting automation?

It is using software to move and check financial data between your shop management system and your books, so sales, payments and parts purchases are not typed in twice. A person still sets it up, reviews it and closes the month.

Can my shop management system send invoices to QuickBooks?

Many can, through a built-in connection or a partner tool. Tekmetric, for example, connects to QuickBooks through The Back Office's Accounting Link and lets you review data before it is sent. Ask your vendor what syncs and how voids and refunds are handled.

What should I reconcile and how often?

Tekmetric's accounting guide suggests checking numbers monthly and reconciling thoroughly each quarter, with parts as the first place to look: compare parts purchased against parts used and match each paid part to a paid ticket.

Does automation replace my bookkeeper or CPA?

No. It removes retyping and flags mismatches. Mapping the accounts, closing the books, tax treatment and sign-off stay with a qualified person.

Is RevvOS accounting software?

No. RevvOS does not post to your books or give financial advice. It surfaces operational exceptions from your shop systems with their source and last-updated time, and it does not treat job totals as collected cash.
