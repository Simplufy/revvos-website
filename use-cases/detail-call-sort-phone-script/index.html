The Detail Call Sort: an auto detailing phone script for your AI receptionist

This auto detailing phone script is the Detail Call Sort: on every detail call your receptionist, human or AI, captures the package the caller means, the condition in their own words, and the other shop's number with what it covered, before anyone quotes. Coating and film questions go to a person, and nobody promises a swirl will come out. Below are the script, the capture checklist and the routing rules to copy.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 9 min read

The Detail Call Sort is three things your phone answering captures on every detail call, in this order, before anyone quotes a price:

  1. The package they mean. Not the word they used, the package on your sheet that matches it.
  2. The condition, in their words. Pet hair, sap, odor, heavy soil, swirls, whatever they describe.
  3. The other shop's number, and what it covered. Most callers have one. The scope behind it matters more than the figure.

Two rules sit on top of the three. Every coating or film question goes to a person, because nobody has looked at the paint yet. And the receptionist never promises that a swirl, scratch or stain will come out.

That's the whole sort. The rest of this page is the setup: who it fits, what to prepare, the script to copy, the checklist, and how to tell it's working.

Why a detail call needs its own sort

Most phone scripts in this trade were written for repair shops. A repair call turns into a line item: a noise, a light, a part. A detail call turns into a package, a vehicle and a description of a condition, and the price lives in that description.

Two SUVs that look alike from the curb can be a quick job or a whole weekend. Pet hair, sap and neglected paint decide which, and none of them come through a phone line clearly. So the receptionist's job, human or AI, is not to price the job. Its job is to collect what a person needs to price it, and to hand that over without promising anything the shop then has to walk back.

If you already use an AI to pick up the phone, the general setup is on our page about AI phone answering for auto shops. This guide is the detail-specific layer on top of it.

Who this is for

This is for detail shops, and for repair or collision shops with a detail bay, that take a fair share of new business by phone. It fits three situations best:

  • You already have an AI receptionist or answering service, and it keeps getting asked "how much for a full detail?"
  • Your front counter answers the phone between jobs and keeps writing down half a call.
  • You lose callers to a cheaper number from another shop and suspect the two quotes weren't for the same work.

It doesn't fit a shop that books everything online at fixed prices and has no phone at all. Most detailers aren't that shop.

What you need before you set it up

Get these four things on one page before you touch any settings. They're what the script reads from.

  1. Your package sheet, in customer words. Each package with its name, what's in it, and the two or three phrases callers use for it ("full detail", "inside and out", "the works").
  2. Your condition list. The conditions that change the job: pet hair, smoke or odor, spills and stains, heavy soil, sap and tar, water spots, swirls and scratches. Mark which ones always mean "a person looks first."
  3. Your routing rules. Who takes a coating or film inquiry, who takes a fleet or dealer account, and who takes a complaint about earlier work. Name a person, not a role, and a backup.
  4. Your text wording. If the receptionist offers to text a confirmation or ask for photos, write that text now, including how the customer says stop.

The phone script to copy

Change the shop name and package names. The order matters more than the exact words.

  1. Greeting. "Thanks for calling [shop name], this is the automated assistant. I can get your details to the team so they can give you a real price. What kind of vehicle is it?"
  2. Package. "Which service were you thinking about?" Then read back the matching package from your sheet: "That sounds like our [package name], which includes [two items]. Is that what you're after?"
  3. Condition. "What's the car like right now? Things like pet hair, stains, sap or marks in the paint all change the job." Write down their words exactly, and don't translate them.
  4. The other number. "Have you had a price from anyone else? If so, what did it include?" Capture the figure and the scope ("wash and wax", "interior only", "one-step polish").
  5. Date and contact. "When do you need it back?" Then the best number, and whether a text is fine.
  6. Close. "Thanks, I've got the vehicle, the service and what it looks like now. [Person's name] will call you back with a price." Never close with a price.

The lines the receptionist never says, whatever the caller asks:

  • "Yes, those swirls will buff right out."
  • "That stain will come out."
  • "Coating starts at [any number]."
  • "You can pick it up tonight," on a coated car.

The capture checklist

Calls that go to a person every time

Coating and film questions. A coating price depends on the paint, and the paint hasn't been seen. XPEL's own guide says installers assess each vehicle's paint individually and give a detailed proposal, and that without paint correction, blemishes and scratches are sealed under the coating and highlighted afterward. It describes three correction stages chosen by need, from no visible defects up to heavy swirls. That's a consult. The receptionist captures the vehicle, what they want the coating to do, and whether the paint has been corrected or repainted, then hands it over.

Anything about scratches coming out. The honest answer depends on depth. Mike Stoops of Meguiar's told AAA that if you can feel the scratch with your fingernail, it may be too deep for an easy fix, while one you can see but can't feel can probably be removed. Nobody can run that test over the phone. So the receptionist says a person will look at it, and stops there.

Fleet and dealer accounts. Volume, cadence and terms are negotiated by an owner or manager. Capture the fleet size, the vehicle types, the sites and who signs the invoice.

Complaints about earlier work. Those go to a person with the photos and the job record in front of them, never to an automated answer.

Promises about time

A coated car doesn't leave on the same rules as a washed one. IGL Coatings tells owners to keep the car dry for at least 24 hours and avoid washing it with chemicals for at least 7 days after install, and says some coatings need a full cure of 2 to 4 days. Detail King's advice to detailers is no washing for the first 7 to 10 days, and to tell clients a coating is protection, "not an invincibility shield."

So the receptionist never gives a pickup time for a coating job. It captures when the customer needs the car back, and the person who books the job decides whether that works with the product's cure.

What to watch out for

  • The receptionist drifting into pricing. Read ten transcripts a week and look for any number it said out loud. One price quoted from a description is one too many.
  • Translated conditions. "Pretty dirty" written down as "standard" hides the job. The checklist asks for their words for a reason.
  • Outbound calls and texts. Answering is one thing. The FCC noted in 2024 that the TCPA's requirements do not extend to technologies used to answer inbound calls, and that its rule on artificial voices covers outbound calls. Calling or texting people back is outbound, so get consent on the call and keep a stop option in every text. Ask your own counsel about your state.
  • Missed calls. The sort only helps on calls that get answered. If calls still ring out, start with missed call automation first.

How to tell it's working

Give it two weeks, then pull every detail call from that stretch and check three things.

  1. Fields filled. For each call, count how many of the seven checklist fields have a real answer. Most calls should have all of them, and the gaps should read "caller didn't know," not "never asked."
  2. Promises made. Search the transcripts for "will come out," "buff out," any price, and any pickup time on a coating. There should be none.
  3. Callbacks ready to quote. Ask whoever returns the calls how many they could price without phoning the customer again for details. That count should climb week over week.

If it doesn't, the fix is almost always the condition question. Make it more specific, add your own examples to it, and run another two weeks.

Questions people ask

Should an AI receptionist quote prices for a detail?

Not from a description. It can read back what a package includes, but the price depends on the condition, which a person has to judge. Capture the package, the condition and any competing quote, then hand it to whoever prices jobs.

What should happen when a caller asks about ceramic coating?

Route it to a person every time. Installers assess each car's paint before quoting, and XPEL notes that skipped correction seals defects under the coating. The receptionist captures the vehicle, the goal and the paint history.

Can the receptionist tell a caller their swirls will buff out?

No. Whether a scratch comes out depends on its depth, and a Meguiar's expert told AAA that one you can feel with a fingernail may be too deep for an easy fix. That's judged in person, so the answer on the phone is that someone will look at it.

What does the other shop's number tell me?

Mostly the scope the caller was quoted. A wash and wax and a paint correction aren't the same job, so capturing what the other quote covered lets you answer the comparison honestly instead of defending a figure.
