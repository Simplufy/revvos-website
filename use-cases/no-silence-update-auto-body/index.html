The No-Silence Update: collision repair customer updates for every stage

The No-Silence Update is a way to send collision repair customer updates at every natural moment of a job, each with three things: the real status, the real reason for any delay, and the next date you'd actually stand behind. Copy the stage table, the message templates, the rule about hopeful dates and the three conversations that need a person below.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

The No-Silence Update is a set of customer messages for collision shops: at every natural moment of the job, send three things, so the customer never sits in silence wondering whether you forgot their car. It is the same single move as the video:

  • At every natural moment, from check-in to teardown, the supplement, paint and ready for delivery, send an update.
  • Each update carries three things: the real status, the real reason for any delay, and the next date you'd actually stand behind.
  • And one rule: never send a hopeful date. A "should be done Friday" that slips is worse than no update at all.

Below are the stages, a message for each one, the three conversations that need a person, and how to tell it's working.

Why silence costs a body shop so much

A collision customer isn't a service customer. They lose their car for days or weeks, they're often in a rental with a due date, an insurer sits in the middle, and nobody but your shop sees the car during the repair. A customer who hears nothing for four days assumes the worst, then calls, and an advisor spends fifteen minutes rebuilding trust one message would have kept.

The jobs are getting longer and more complicated, which means more days to fill. CCC's Crash Course data, as reported by Autobody News, shows repair days up four to five days relative to 2020, calibrations on more than 31% of DRP estimates and nearly 87% of DRP estimates including a scan. The same report notes that increased supplement handling may be stretching repair times. Our longer page on auto body customer update automation covers the background.

Who it is for

  • Independent collision shops whose advisors spend the day returning "any update?" calls.
  • Multi-location groups that want every customer to get the same updates at the same stages.
  • Shops that have been burned by a promised date that slipped.

What you need first

  • Your repair stages as your shop management system records them.
  • A person (usually the advisor) who approves each update before it goes out.
  • The customer's preferred channel (text or email) and their consent to receive updates.
  • The rental due date, if there is one.

The three things in every update

  1. The real status. Where the car actually is today, in plain words. If it's waiting on a supplement approval, say so. Customers accept waiting. They don't accept finding out they were told something softer than the truth.
  2. The real reason for any delay. A backordered part, a sublet, an approval still pending, a booth queue. Naming the reason is what stops an anxious customer from assuming you forgot their car.
  3. The next date you'd stand behind. Often that isn't the finish date. "We'll update you Thursday by noon" is a date you can keep. "Should be done Friday" usually isn't.

The stages and a message for each

The article behind this guide lists nine natural update moments. Here are the messages to copy (names and details are demo examples).

Stage What the update says
Checked in The car is here, who has it, what happens next
Teardown found more What was found, what it means, that it's normal to find more
Supplement submitted Extra work went to the insurer, who decides it
Insurer approved The work is moving, and the revised window
In paint Where it is, and the next date
In reassembly Any open part or sublet, named
In calibration A planned step for their vehicle
In QC It's being checked before it's called done
Ready, delivery set Pickup time, payment, rental return

Checked in: Hi Dana, your CR-V is checked in at Redline Auto Elm St, and Priya is your advisor. Next we take apart the damaged area to see everything that needs repair. We'll update you by Wednesday at noon.

Teardown found more: Hi Dana, once we took the bumper off we found a bent bracket behind it. That's common in rear hits, and it means more work than the first estimate. We're sending it to your insurer now.

Supplement submitted: Hi Dana, we sent the extra repairs to your insurer for approval. They make that decision, not us. We'll update you Thursday by noon, whether or not we've heard back.

In calibration: Hi Dana, your CR-V is having its safety cameras and sensors calibrated. This is a planned step for your vehicle after this repair, not an extra surprise.

Ready, delivery set: Hi Dana, your CR-V is ready. Priya will walk you around it at 4:30 today. Bring your rental keys and we'll help you return it.

Calibrations really are becoming routine. Repairer Driven News, reporting on CCC data, notes that vehicles with driver assistance systems demand more frequent and complex diagnostics, so a calibration update heard as a planned step beats a line item discovered at pickup.

Never send a hopeful date

You'd think a hopeful date keeps a customer calm. It does, until it slips. An update that says "should be done Friday" and then isn't done Friday gives the customer a broken promise to be angry about, which is worse than no update at all. Keep three kinds of dates apart:

  • Scheduled: something you control and have booked, like a booth slot.
  • Waiting: something someone else controls, like an insurer approval or a part delivery. Give the date of your next update, not a finish date.
  • Hoped for: keep it to yourself.

Three conversations that need a person

Automation can carry the routine updates. It hands off the moment a message stops being routine:

  • A total loss. More cars are being totaled; CCC data cited by Autobody News shows 22.6% of claims were total losses through April 2025. A person delivers that news in their own words.
  • A delay caused by a supplement dispute. That's between the shop and the insurer, and a person explains it.
  • A customer unhappy with the result. A finish or fit complaint is a relationship moment, not a template.

Also keep fault and coverage out of every message. Coverage depends on the customer's policy: collision coverage, as the Insurance Information Institute puts it, reimburses you for damage to your car that occurs as a result of a collision, and the insurer decides what it pays.

How to tell it worked

Pull your last ten closed collision jobs and count, for each one, the updates the customer got and the longest stretch with none. Then turn on the stage messages for a month and count again, along with the "any update?" calls your front desk logs. You can follow CCC's Crash Course reports for industry context, but your own call log is the benchmark that matters. Questions people ask

How often should a body shop update a collision customer?

At every natural moment of the job, such as check-in, teardown, supplement, approval, paint, reassembly, calibration, quality check and ready for delivery, and never with a gap long enough for them to wonder whether you forgot the car.

Should an update give a completion date?

Only a date you'd stand behind. If the job is waiting on an insurer or a part, give the date of your next update instead of a hopeful finish date.

What should never be sent automatically?

A total loss, a supplement dispute, a complaint about the repair, anything about fault, and any completion date the shop hasn't confirmed. A person handles those.

Why mention calibration in an update?

Because more repairs need scans and calibrations, and a customer who hears it as a planned step for their vehicle is less surprised than one who finds it at pickup.
