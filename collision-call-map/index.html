The Collision Call Map: a body shop phone call checklist for people and AI receptionists

This body shop phone call checklist sorts every call a collision shop gets into seven types and gives each one two lines: what must be captured before the call ends, and what has to reach a person while the caller is still on the line. That's the Collision Call Map. It works for a human front desk and for an AI receptionist. Below are the seven call types, the three triggers that always put a person on, the three things the phone never promises, and a one-week test to run on your own calls.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

Who this is for

This checklist is for collision and auto body shops, from a single location to a group, that are training a front desk or looking at an AI receptionist. It goes with our short video on the Collision Call Map and the longer page on AI receptionists for body shops.

The reason it exists is simple: a body shop's phone isn't a mechanical shop's phone. It's an adjuster asking about a supplement, a tow driver at the gate and a customer on day nine of a rental. A receptionist built for oil changes treats every call the same, and that's where it fails.

Timing makes it harder. Podium's Brett Steele told Repairer Driven News that 36% of collision repair customers try to contact a shop outside regular hours. That's a 2019 figure from a messaging vendor, so treat it as directional, but check your own after-hours voicemail.

What you need first

  • A week of calls. Recordings if your phone system keeps them (with the disclosure your state requires), or a tally sheet at the counter if it doesn't.
  • Names for the hand-offs. Who takes adjuster calls, who takes an upset customer, who owns DRP referrals, who takes total-loss questions.
  • Your shop system open. The front desk needs to see the vehicle's status to route a status call correctly.

The seven call types

Each call type has a different required outcome. Two columns matter: what to capture, and what goes to a person while the caller is still on the line.

# Call type Capture before the call ends A person takes it when
1 New accident Drivable, anyone hurt, where the car is, who else is involved, insurer notified, a working callback number Any injury, any question about fault, any tow the shop doesn't control
2 DRP or insurer referral Insurer, claim number, policyholder or insurer calling, the adjuster's name, whether the car is directed here The insurer asks the shop to accept or decline a job, or it's after hours
3 Status call from a customer in a rental The vehicle, and the exact question (part arrived, supplement approved, done Friday) Second or third call about the same car, or the repair is past its promised date
4 Adjuster or appraiser Who, which claim, which vehicle, what they need Always: an adjuster call is never a message
5 Parts or sublet vendor Which job, which part or sublet, the ETA, price change or substitution A damaged part, a sublet that can't be honored, a price change against an approved estimate
6 Total-loss or diminished-value question Whether the car is here, the claim number, whether they've been told it's a total loss Always, and to someone with authority
7 Tow arrival Which car, which truck, what time, whether anyone expects it A car with no record, or damage that isn't on the ticket

The vendor call (type 5) is the one shops lose most quietly: a glass vendor calling with a date usually reaches whoever is at the counter, and nobody writes it down. Put it in the record, attached to the car.

Three triggers that always put a person on

Whatever the caller asked, these move the call to a person right away.

Fault or legal

Who was at fault, whether to call a lawyer, what the police report says. Capture the facts as stated and transfer. Never comment or agree, because the shop's words on fault can end up in a claim file.

An upset customer whose repair ran long

Language about lost time, rental cost or a promise someone made. Apologize for the wait, confirm the vehicle and callback number, and hand the call over before the customer has to repeat themselves.

An insurer negotiation

Any call asking the shop to change, justify or accept something: a labor rate, a parts choice, a supplement line, a total-loss threshold. Identify the claim and route it to whoever owns that relationship. No automated system agrees to anything.

Three things the phone never promises

  1. A repair price. No figure, range or ballpark for a repair, paint, a part or a sublet. A collision estimate changes after teardown, and the numbers show why: CCC data reported by Repairer Driven News found that 56.5% of Q1 2025 DRP calibrations appeared on a supplement, not on the first estimate.
  2. A completion date. Not even "probably Friday". The same report found repairs with multiple calibrations averaged more than 17 days keys to keys, against 13 days with none, so a date given at intake depends on things nobody knows yet.
  3. Insurance or legal advice. Whether to file, whether to accept a settlement, what a policy covers. That comes from the insurer or the customer's own advisor.

You'd think a quick ballpark keeps people happy. It does, until the number moves, and then it's the promise the customer holds you to.

Phone lines to copy

Status call, repair on schedule:
"Thanks for calling Redline Auto Body. Let me pull up your car. I can see it's in repair; I'll have Luis call you with the details on the parts today."

Status call, second call or past the date:
"I'm sorry you've had to call again. Let me confirm the car and the best number, and I'll get Luis on the line now."

Price question before teardown:
"I can't give a price over the phone, because a collision estimate changes once the car is taken apart. We can get it in for an estimate; what day works?"

The names are examples. Replace them with your shop and your people.

If the receptionist is AI

The same map is the specification. An AI receptionist should answer, capture, book a slot for a person to confirm, attach the message to the right car and route; it should never decide. Tell callers they're speaking with an automated assistant. If you ever use an AI voice for outbound calls, the FCC's February 2024 ruling treats AI-generated voices as artificial under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which brings consent rules with it.

And make the callback work. Hiya's 2026 report says 86% of calls from unknown numbers go unanswered, so when a person calls a customer back, a text first with the shop's name helps the call get picked up.

The one-week test

Take a week of calls and sort each one into the seven types. Mark the ones a person should have taken and didn't, and the ones where someone gave a price or a date. That list is your specification: it tells you what to train, and if you're buying an AI receptionist, what to test it on before it takes a single live call.

Questions people ask

What is the Collision Call Map?

A checklist that sorts body shop calls into seven types and gives each one what to capture and when a person takes over, plus three triggers and three things the phone never promises.

Should a body shop give a price over the phone?

No. A collision estimate changes after teardown, and many calibrations only appear on a supplement, so a phone price becomes a promise that moves.

Which calls should always go to a person?

Adjuster and appraiser calls, total-loss and diminished-value questions, anything about fault, an upset customer whose repair ran long, and any insurer negotiation.

Can an AI receptionist handle collision calls?

It can answer, capture the claim and the car, book a slot for a person to confirm and route the call. It should never quote, promise a date or give insurance or legal advice.
