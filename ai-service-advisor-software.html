AI service advisor software: what it should do at the counter

AI service advisor software should take the writing and remembering off an advisor's plate: turning tech notes into plain language, drafting estimate follow-ups and status texts, tracking declined work and callbacks. The advisor keeps the judgment: what to recommend, what it costs, and how to deliver bad news.

Updated October 14, 2026 · 7 min read

AI service advisor software should take the writing and remembering off an advisor's plate, not the selling. It turns a tech's notes into a plain-language explanation, drafts the follow-up on an estimate that went quiet, writes the status text when a part arrives, and keeps the list of who is owed a callback. The advisor still decides what to recommend, what it costs and how to say the hard part.

That split matters because the advisor is the person the customer actually trusts. Software that makes them faster helps. Software that talks over them hurts.

What a service advisor's day actually involves

Watch a busy advisor for an hour and the work falls into two piles.

The first pile is judgment and conversation: greeting the customer, hearing the symptom, deciding which of the tech's findings matter today, presenting the estimate, handling "can we do half now?", and making the call when the job runs long.

The second pile is paperwork and memory: typing up the concern, translating "LF inner tie rod has excessive play" into something a customer understands, chasing the estimate from last Thursday, texting status updates, remembering that the customer declined rear brakes at 3 mm in April, and logging all of it in the shop-management system.

Good AI service advisor software lives almost entirely in the second pile. Shops already reach for it there: in the Ratchet+Wrench 2026 industry survey, service advisor scripts were the most common use among shops that named an AI tool (27%), ahead of scheduling tools (17%).

Why the advisor's job is worth protecting

Communication is where shops win or lose customers. Cox Automotive's 2025 service study found 45% of vehicle owners were dissatisfied with dealership service, mainly because of unexpected costs and poor communication. Those are advisor moments. An advisor buried in follow-up texts has less attention for them.

The labor market won't make this easier. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects about 66,200 openings a year for automotive technicians and mechanics through 2035, and in a shop short on techs, the advisor often ends up covering the gap.

Six advisor tasks AI can help with today

Turning tech notes into customer language

What AI does: reads the inspection notes and drafts a short explanation per item: what was found, why it matters, and whether it's urgent or can wait. Photos from the digital inspection stay attached.

What the advisor does: checks the draft against what the tech actually said and decides the order. A worn belt and a leaking brake line should not read with the same urgency.

Photos matter here. A write-up of J.D. Power's 2026 Customer Service Index results reports that 64% of dealer service customers want photo or video evidence of recommended work, but far fewer receive it. A clear sentence next to each photo is exactly the kind of writing AI can draft.

Building the follow-up on open estimates

What AI does: spots estimates with no decision after a set number of days and drafts a follow-up that names the work and the vehicle. "Your 2017 Camry's front brakes" lands better than "your recent estimate."

What the advisor does: decides who gets a text, who gets a call and who gets left alone. The advisor knows the customer said "after payday."

Status updates during the repair

What AI does: drafts a text when the repair order changes stage: parts arrived, car in final inspection, ready for pickup.

What the advisor does: handles anything that's bad news. Additional work found, a delay, a part that came in wrong. Those are calls, not texts.

Remembering declined work

What AI does: keeps the list of declined items with the date and the measurement (pads at 3 mm, tires at 4/32) and brings it up when the customer books next, or when the item is likely due.

What the advisor does: decides whether to raise it, and how.

Callbacks and messages

What AI does: collects every promised callback, voicemail and text that needs a reply into one list with a due time and the customer's history attached.

What the advisor does: makes the calls, in the order that makes sense.

Preparing for the next visit

What AI does: before a returning customer arrives, pulls a short note: last visit, open recommendations, any complaint on file, and whether they left a review.

What the advisor does: uses it or ignores it. The point is that nobody has to dig.

What AI should not do for an advisor

  • Set prices or discounts. Even a "small" automatic discount changes your margins and sets an expectation.
  • Send messages without a person approving them, at least until you've reviewed a few hundred drafts and trust the pattern.
  • Diagnose. It can summarize what the tech found. It should not suggest what's wrong with the car.
  • Handle complaints. A frustrated customer wants a person who can fix it.

If a tool you're evaluating does any of these by default, ask how to turn it off.

How dealer tools and independent-shop tools differ

Dealer service departments get AI from their dealer management system vendors. CDK, for example, describes an AI virtual assistant that books service appointments by phone, chat or text, plus smart replies, translations and sentiment alerts for customer messages. Those tools assume a large service lane with a dedicated BDC.

Independent shops usually run a cloud shop-management system and add tools around it. The questions are the same, but the bar for setup is lower: one or two advisors can't spend a week configuring software.

How to judge a tool in a 30-minute demo

  1. Ask it to read a real repair order. If it can't see your shop-management data, it can only draft generic text.
  2. Ask where each draft goes. To the advisor's queue for approval, or straight to the customer?
  3. Ask what happens when data is missing. A good tool says "no parts ETA on file." A bad one guesses.
  4. Count the clicks to approve a draft. If approving takes longer than writing, advisors will stop using it.
  5. Ask what gets logged. You want a record of what was suggested, what the advisor changed and what was sent.
  6. Ask who owns customer data and recordings. Get the answer in writing.

The Federal Trade Commission has been clear that there is no AI exemption from consumer protection law. Anything the software tells a customer in your name is your shop's statement.

Questions people ask

What software do service advisors use?

Most advisors work inside a shop-management system (independents) or a dealer management system (dealers), plus a digital inspection tool and a texting tool. AI service advisor software usually sits on top of those and drafts work for the advisor.

Can AI replace a service advisor?

No. AI can handle the paperwork and memory parts of the job, but customers buy repairs from a person they trust. The advisor's judgment on what to recommend and how to present it is the part software should support, not replace.

Should AI send follow-up texts to customers automatically?

We recommend starting with drafts that an advisor approves. Once you've reviewed enough drafts to trust the pattern, you can decide whether any low-risk message types, such as a ready-for-pickup text, should go out on their own.

Does AI service advisor software work with Tekmetric or Shopmonkey?

It depends on the tool and the connection. Shop-management vendors control API access, so ask each vendor exactly which data it reads and writes in your system before you buy.

What should I ask in a demo of AI advisor software?

Ask it to read one of your real repair orders, show where drafts go, show what happens when data is missing, and count the clicks needed to approve a draft. Also get in writing who owns your customer data.
