AI software for auto shops: eight questions to ask before you buy

To choose AI software for an auto shop, start with one job (such as estimate follow-up) and ask every vendor eight questions: which system it reads, what it can change, who approves customer messages, what it does when data is missing, what record it keeps, how it handles calls and texts legally, what proof it has, and the all-in price.

Updated October 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Before you sign anything, ask the vendor eight questions about your own shop. Which system does it read? Can it write back? Who approves what it sends? What happens when the feed is down? A good vendor answers each one in a sentence, without slides. If the answers are vague, the product probably is too.

We sell AI software to auto shops, so read this with that in mind. The questions work for any vendor, including us.

Start with the job, not the product

"AI software for auto shops" covers very different products. A phone agent, a review-reply tool, a declined-work follow-up tool and a multi-shop reporting layer have little in common besides the label. Write down the one job you want done, the person who does it today, and how you'd know it got better. That sentence is your test for every demo.

Good jobs to start with are ones that are repetitive, have a clear owner and leave a trail in your shop-management system: open estimates, unanswered reviews, callbacks owed, parts delays.

The eight questions to ask any vendor

1. Which system does it read, and how?

Shop-management systems decide who gets in. Tekmetric, for example, lists 70+ partner integrations and handles API access through an application process. Ask whether the vendor has an approved connection to your system, a workaround such as exports, or only a plan. Ask to see your own data in the demo, not a sample shop.

2. Can it write back, and what exactly can it change?

Reading an estimate is low risk. Changing a repair order, a price or an appointment is not. Get a list of every field it can write, and who switched that on.

3. Who approves what it sends to customers?

Look for a named person per action type: the service advisor for estimate follow-ups, the manager for review replies, the owner for refunds. If the answer is "the AI handles it", ask what happens when it promises the wrong price.

4. What does it do when data is missing or a feed is down?

This is the question vendors least expect. A safe system shows the gap and waits. An unsafe one fills it in with a guess. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, a voluntary federal guide released in January 2023, organizes this kind of thinking into four functions: govern, map, measure and manage. You don't need to read it to ask the question, but serious vendors will know it.

5. What record is left afterward?

You want to see the original data, the draft, who approved it, and when. Without that record, nobody can sort out a customer complaint or a wrong charge later.

6. Does it call or text customers, and is that legal in your state?

Voice tools need care. In February 2024 the FCC ruled unanimously that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, the law that limits robocalls. Answering inbound calls is a different situation from placing outbound ones, but ask the vendor how consent is handled for every outbound call and text.

7. Can they show you real results, and are they labeled honestly?

Ask for a customer you can call. Representative or demo numbers are fine if they are labeled. Unlabeled ones are a red flag, and regulators agree: in 2024 the FTC brought five cases in one sweep over deceptive AI claims, with the chair saying there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books.

8. What does it cost, all in?

Per-location fees, per-message fees, setup work and connection costs add up. Get the number for your shop count and your systems, in writing, before the pilot.

Why communication tools come up first

Most shops start with customer follow-up because that is where the complaints come from. In Cox Automotive's 2025 survey of 1,974 vehicle owners, 45% were unhappy with dealership service, mostly over unexpected costs and poor communication, and 55% said comparing costs online was very important. Independent shops are picking up those customers. The shops that keep them are the ones that call back when they said they would.

That is a good first workflow for AI software: it is measurable, it already has an owner (the advisor), and the risk of a draft that a person reviews is low.

Questions people ask

What is the best AI software for an auto repair shop?

There is no single best tool, because products do different jobs: phones, estimate follow-up, reviews, reporting or back office. The best one is the tool that does your most expensive job, connects to your shop-management system and lets a person approve customer-facing actions.

Does AI software work with Tekmetric or Shopmonkey?

Some tools do, but shop-management systems control access to their data. Ask the vendor whether they have an approved connection to your system or only a plan, and ask to see your own data in the demo.

Is it legal for AI to call my customers?

The FCC ruled in 2024 that AI-generated voices count as artificial voices under the TCPA, so outbound calls carry the same consent rules as robocalls. Ask any vendor how consent is handled, and check your state's rules.

Should AI software send messages without approval?

For low-stakes reminders some shops allow it. For estimates, prices, refunds and public review replies, we recommend a named person approves each message, because mistakes there cost money and trust.

How long does it take to set up AI software in a shop?

It depends on the job and how your systems connect. A focused pilot on one workflow is a sensible start; RevvOS runs a 45-day pilot across one to three shops.
