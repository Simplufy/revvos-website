The Four-Reason Sort: declined work follow up with a message for each reason

Declined work follow up starts with one question your advisor answers at the counter: why did the customer say not today? The Four-Reason Sort gives every declined line one of four reasons (money, timing, second opinion or a real no) plus an urgency, and each reason gets its own next step and its own message. Below are the reason codes, the ten-second entry, the timing rules, four message templates to copy, the mistakes to avoid, and how to count whether it's working.

Updated October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Who this is for

This sheet is for service advisors, managers and owners who have a pile of declined estimates in the shop system and no clear way to follow up without sounding pushy. It goes with our short video on the Four-Reason Sort and the longer article on declined work follow up software.

The idea from the video: think of a declined line like a returned part. Nothing goes back on the shelf until someone writes why it came back. A declined line without a reason is a dead end; a declined line with a reason is a follow-up you can time.

It matters because declining is a normal week now. PartsTech's 2026 survey of nearly 700 shops found 50% said customers are delaying or declining work, and 49% said customers are more cautious about non-required work than the year before.

What you need first

  • A place to store the reason. Most shops already have one. Ratchet+Wrench's 2026 survey found electronic management system use at 59%, and nearly every shop-management system has a notes or reason field on a declined line. Use the same four words every time.
  • Agreement at the counter. Every advisor uses the same four codes. Show them this sheet.
  • A named person who approves the messages. Usually the advisor who wrote the estimate.

The four reasons, in order

Your advisor picks one when the customer says it. It takes about ten seconds, and software can't guess it later.

1. Money

The customer wants the work done but can't pay today. Next step: offer to split the work into phases, safety first, and tie the rest to a later visit. Don't lead with a discount.

2. Timing

Wrong week, the car is needed, a trip is coming. Next step: ask for a date and send the reminder on that date. The customer named it, so the reminder is a favor, not a sales call.

3. Second opinion

They're shopping the quote. Next step: send one note offering to answer questions or show the photo. One note, then wait.

4. No

They don't believe it's needed, or they're selling the car. Next step: mark it closed and leave it closed. This is the reason that protects your reputation, because a shop that keeps texting after a firm no confirms what wary drivers already suspect. In AAA's survey, two out of three drivers said they don't trust repair shops in general, and 76% of those cited being sold unnecessary services. That survey is from 2016, so treat it as background rather than a current measure.

Add one more field: urgency

A seeping valve cover gasket and a brake pad at 2 mm are both declined, but they aren't the same conversation. Add a second field with three values:

Urgency Example How you follow up
Safety Brake pads near minimum, a tire with cords showing Mention it first, with the photo, at the reason's time
Soon A leak that will get worse, worn struts Bring it up at the next visit or the reason's date
Comfort Cabin filter, a cosmetic repair Only at the next visit, never on its own

When to send each one

  • Money: near the next visit or a date the customer suggests, with the phased plan.
  • Timing: on the date they named. If they didn't name one, ask once.
  • Second opinion: one note within a couple of days, then nothing unless they reply.
  • No: never.

Tie every reminder to the car when you can. The next oil change, an inspection due date or seasonal prep are natural moments, because the customer is coming back anyway. And flag stale items: a recommendation from eight months ago may no longer match the car, so the advisor should re-inspect rather than re-quote blindly.

Four messages to copy

The names below are examples. Replace them with your customer, your car and your item. Each message names the specific item, comes from a person and gives one easy next step.

Money (phased):

Hi Mr. Hollis, it's Priya at Redline Auto. When your Civic was in, the rear brake pads were at about 3 mm. We could do the pads now and leave the rotors for your next visit, so it's a smaller bill today. Want me to price that out?

Timing (their date):

Hi Ms. Ortiz, it's Priya at Redline Auto. You mentioned you'd be back from your trip around the 14th. Want me to hold a slot that week for the front struts on the Tacoma?

Second opinion (one note):

Hi Mr. Nguyen, it's Priya at Redline Auto. If you have any questions on the timing cover leak we found on your RAV4, I'm happy to walk you through the photo. No pressure either way.

No (closed): no message. Note the reason, for example "selling the car", so nobody on the team follows up by mistake.

Compare those with the kind of text that backfires: "Hi! You have declined services. Book now for a discount!" It names nothing, comes from nobody, and a coupon on declined work teaches customers to decline.

Rules around texting

If software sends the texts, Twilio's A2P 10DLC documentation explains the registration that carriers expect for business texting on 10DLC numbers, and your own lawyer should confirm the consent you need. Keep a record of when and why you contacted each customer. If a customer asks you to stop, stop in every channel, and mark the line closed.

How to tell it's working

Count your own numbers for 60 days, starting today: declined lines with a reason code, lines followed up, and lines later approved. Don't use a recovery percentage from a vendor page; nobody else's baseline tells you about your shop. Two quick signs it's working before the count is in: advisors stop asking "what was this one about?" when they open an old estimate, and no customer gets a reminder after saying no.

Questions people ask

What is the Four-Reason Sort?

A way to follow up on declined repair work: every declined line gets one of four reasons (money, timing, second opinion or no) and an urgency, and each reason has its own timing and message.

Should I offer a discount on declined work?

Usually not. A coupon on declined work teaches customers to decline and wait for the next one. For money reasons, offer to split the work into phases instead.

How many times should I follow up on a declined repair?

It depends on the reason. Money and timing get one reminder at the right moment, a second opinion gets one note, and a firm no gets none.

What if the recommendation is months old?

Flag it as stale and have the advisor re-inspect at the next visit instead of re-sending the old quote, because the car may have changed.
